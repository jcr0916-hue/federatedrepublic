Constitutional Question Presented

Whether a certified whole-Territory statehood petition under §15.2 affects a "different defined portion of the same Territory" from a certified corridor merger petition under §15.4, within the meaning of §15.5.a(1) — such that the Joint Monitor Council correctly determined the corridor referendum could not proceed and the Civic Consul was required to convene a Territory Convention under §15.5.a(4).

Holding

Yes. A whole-Territory petition affects a "different defined portion" of the Territory from a corridor petition for purposes of §15.5.a(1), because the two claims describe incompatible dispositions of the same ground. The Joint Monitor Council's determination is affirmed. The Territory Convention continues. The interim stay of NRS-Y13-0671 is dissolved; the ninety-day period established under §15.5.a(4) resumes running as of this order, credited for the three days that had elapsed before the stay took effect.

Opinion of the Court

Justice Ola Vrentis
Joined by Justices Aldren, Corse, Halvath, Imen, and Mevra

We address jurisdiction before anything else, because it is contested and because our dissenting colleague would end the matter there. Plaintiffs frame this as a challenge to the Joint Monitor Council's certification and determination function, bringing it within our original jurisdiction under §4.5(5) over "constitutional challenges involving constitutionally independent institutions where their independence protections make inferior court review structurally inappropriate." The dissent observes, correctly, that §11.1 reserves the phrase "constitutionally independent panels" for the NRS Panel and the Elections Panel by name, while §9.7 describes the JMC only as the "standing coordination body" the three Monitor Generals jointly constitute. From this the dissent would conclude that a challenge to a JMC determination is not, without more, a challenge to a "constitutionally independent institution" in the sense §4.5(5) means.

We do not think the label carries that much weight. The JMC's independence is not freestanding — it is the independence of the three Monitor Generals, each separately protected under Article IX, acting jointly on a matter that by its nature crosses their individual domains. A determination under §15.5.a(1) is not the kind of finding any single Monitor could make alone; it required exactly the joint capacity §9.7 exists to provide. To hold that the Monitor Generals' independence protections evaporate the moment they act together, rather than separately, would create an odd asymmetry: the same three officials, doing the same kind of constitutional work, would be reviewable on different terms depending on whether they filed one finding or three. We do not read §4.5(5) to turn on that formality. Jurisdiction is proper.

Having reached the merits, we turn to whether "the whole of Korda" is a "different defined portion" from "the Kelvant-Korda corridor" within §15.5.a(1). Plaintiffs argue, with real textual force, that a "portion" of a thing is ordinarily understood as less than the whole of it. We do not dismiss that intuition, but the provision does not ask whether a petition claims less than the whole Territory. It asks whether the petition affects a "different defined portion" — and "defined" is doing the operative work. What §15.2's own subdivision clause requires of a subdivision petition is that it identify, with particularity, the ground it claims; a whole-Territory petition does exactly that, at a larger scope. A claim to everything is not an absence of a defined portion. It is the largest one available.

Read for its purpose, the provision confirms this. Subsections (2) and (3) of §15.5.a assume a single petition proceeding on its own track. Subsection (1) exists to displace that ordinary sequence precisely when two certified petitions cannot both be given effect — when granting one forecloses the other. A referendum merging the corridor into Varek, and a Statehood Audit that would make the whole of Korda, corridor included, a single State, are not claims that happen to overlap at the edges. They are two mutually exclusive futures for the same ground, and more completely in conflict than the overlapping-subdivision case the provision plainly reaches. We see no reason the drafters meant subsection (1) to catch a partial conflict but not a total one.

Structurally, §15.5.a treats §15.4 and §15.2 petitions as equals — neither section is given priority, and subsection (4) addresses "all affected portions together" without regard to which petition certified first. Plaintiffs' reading would let a narrower petition, scheduled first, insulate itself from a broader competing claim by the accident of timing. Nothing in Article XV suggests that scheduling should decide which of two legitimate claims prevails.

We are not unmoved by what this costs corridor residents, who organized, petitioned, and were weeks from a vote before this suit was filed — a vote already unwound by the JMC's determination, not by anything we do today. That the inland districts have no vote at all under §15.4's own terms is the structural asymmetry that produced their petition in the first place. Neither claim is illegitimate. §15.5.a(4) exists because the Constitution anticipated that both could be true at once and declined to rank them; that is a choice already made in the text, not a gap for this Court to fill with its own preference.

The Joint Monitor Council's determination is affirmed. The Territory Convention convened under NRS-Y13-0641 continues on its existing terms.

Disposition
The petition for a declaration that §15.5.a(1) was not properly triggered, and the alternative request to restore the corridor referendum to the calendar, are denied. The Joint Monitor Council's determination is affirmed. The interim stay of NRS-Y13-0671 is dissolved; the ninety-day period under §15.5.a(4) resumes running as of this order, credited for the three days elapsed before the stay took effect. Costs to Plaintiffs.

Dissenting Opinion

Justice Pael Doran
Joined by Justices Careth and Norven

I would not reach the question the majority spends most of its opinion answering, because I am not persuaded we have any business answering it on this record.

§4.5(5) grants us original jurisdiction over challenges to "constitutionally independent institutions where their independence protections make inferior court review structurally inappropriate." Two panels bear that label expressly — §11.1 calls the NRS Panel and the Elections Panel "constitutionally independent panels" by name. The JMC is described differently, in a different Article, in different terms: §9.7 constitutes it as "the standing coordination body" the three Monitor Generals form when a matter crosses their domains. It is not a third office created alongside the two named panels. It is a joint session of three offices that already exist.

The majority answers that the Monitor Generals' individual independence carries through when they act jointly, and there is something to that. But it is an answer, not an absence of a question, and the majority reaches it in three paragraphs before spending the rest of the opinion on a merits question I do not think we needed to reach at all today. If Plaintiffs' real grievance is with a finding the Monitor Generals made in their individual, Article IX capacities — which is what a §15.5.a(1) determination substantively is, whatever body's letterhead it went out under — the more careful course is to ask whether the ordinary channels for challenging a Monitor finding were available to them, and were not, before assuming a joint session of three independent officials automatically inherits a fourth, freestanding independence protection under §4.5(5) that the text nowhere gives it in those words.

I do not say the majority's answer is unreasonable. I say we should not need it. This case did not arrive to us on the kind of clock that forecloses careful sequencing — the referendum plaintiffs sought to preserve had already been unwound by the time this suit was filed. Nothing but our own preference to be finished with this required reaching the merits today rather than remanding the jurisdictional question for full development first.

Were I persuaded jurisdiction properly lies here, I would have little quarrel with the majority's textual point that a "defined portion" need not be smaller than the whole to be a portion in the sense §15.5.a(1) requires. That is not the question I am answering. I would resolve the threshold question first, and I do not think this record lets us skip it.
