Verentum · Election Season

The Consul Who Isn't on the Ballot

Elin Thoss sets out what she wants from the rest of this Assembly session and the next one, and why she does not intend to campaign for either.

Petra VendThe TorenthianYear 14, Month 1

VERENTUM — Elin Thoss is the only person in the Republic's executive whose job depends on the spring election without her name appearing anywhere on it. The Civic Consul is chosen by the Assembly, not by voters, and she keeps the office until a constructive vote of no confidence names a successor by an absolute majority. Five hundred Assembly seats are being contested this spring. Her working count stands at 266, fifteen above the line.

This week her office published a short statement of domestic priorities, divided into two halves: what she wants from the rest of this session, and what she intends to propose to the next one. She agreed to talk about it on one condition, which was that we not call it a campaign interview.

Vend: Why publish priorities now, with an election coming?

Thoss: Because the Assembly is still sitting, and it has work in front of it. The statement says what the civic side of the government will ask for before this session ends and what it will propose to the next one. People are entitled to know that before they vote. That isn't the same as asking for their votes.

Vend: You aren't on the ballot. Isn't this election still about you?

Thoss: I serve at the Assembly's pleasure, and I serve the whole country. I'll focus on ideas and policies specifically during the lead-up to the election. The people choose my bosses, not me.

The line is careful. Under §2.6 she is accountable to the Assembly, and the spring vote will decide who sits in it. If her allies lose seats, she does not lose office by that fact alone. Someone else would have to assemble 251 votes for a named successor.

Vend: Start with fiscal equalization. The Joint Committee has been reviewing the formula since Year 13. What do you want?

Thoss: A report, before this session ends. My ministries have drafted a statutory correction and anyone on the Committee or in the Assembly can have it. I can't introduce it and I can't vote for it. The Legislature writes the formula, and the Executive Monitor decides whether it works. My job was to stop the review being something people complained about in private and make it a public process. It's public now. I'd like it finished.

Vend: And if it isn't?

Thoss: Then the next Assembly inherits the file, with every submission still on the record. That's not failure. It's slower than I'd like.

Vend: The Northern Arrival Housing Capacity Act is still in committee.

Thoss: I support it and I'd like it passed this session. Northern communities have been carrying a reception burden for a long time. The bill funds permanent housing and reception capacity. I won't pretend I agree with every line of every draft. When it reaches me I'll say so in writing, as the Constitution provides.

She declined to say which lines she meant.

Vend: Your statement promises monthly backlog figures for Social State claims. Why now?

Thoss: Because people who are waiting for a claim decision deserve to know how long the queue is, and the ministries can publish that without asking anyone's permission. It's within the authority I already have. I'd rather be judged on a number I publish than on one somebody estimates for me.

Vend: The second half of the statement is for an Assembly that doesn't exist yet.

Thoss: That's deliberate. Civil service appointments and promotions should not depend on who holds this office. I came into it through a no-confidence vote; I know exactly how fast an office can change hands. The people who administer the government should be the same people the morning after. That requires statute, so it's a proposal for the next session, not a promise from me.

Vend: And Korda?

Thoss: The Convention did its work and the affected voters will decide. My role was to make sure the process ran as the Constitution sets it out. I don't have a preferred map and I wouldn't tell you if I did.

Some of Thoss's coalition partners have complained privately that her restraint leaves them defending a record she will not defend herself. Asked about that, she paused.

Vend: Your allies say they'd like you louder.

Thoss: They're welcome to be loud. They're candidates. I'm not, and §8.2 doesn't let me turn this office into a campaign. My work this spring is to give the Assembly a record worth deciding about. Whoever sits there after the election can decide what to do with me.

Her statement is published to the National Record System. The Assembly has not yet scheduled a report date for the Joint Committee.
