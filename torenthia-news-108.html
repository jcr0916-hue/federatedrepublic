The Convention · Korda

Twenty-Eight for a Question

The Convention reached its decision. The people who must live with it have not yet reached theirs.

Mara IsetThe Korda Press·KordaYear 13, Month 12

The clerk read the names and the total: twenty-eight in favor, five against. Then, for a moment, the thirty-three-member Korda Territory Convention was quiet enough that the sound of papers being gathered carried into the public gallery.

The result was wider than many participants had expected during the weeks when delegates argued that the two certified petitions could not be reconciled. It did not mean thirty-three people had become one faction. The five opponents included those who believed the canceled full-corridor petition had been diminished and those who believed any partition of Korda was wrong. Among the twenty-eight were delegates who agreed about the next constitutional step while still disagreeing about the preferred eventual future.

The certified Convention record adopts the Northern Lake Settlement as a proposed resolution under §15.5.a(4). The resolution would put three northern districts, including Varenne Station, on a path toward merger with Kelvant. The southern lakefront and the remainder of Korda would remain together; the continuing Territory could pursue the Statehood Audits it already sought, on its eventual boundaries and without waived standards.

No border moved when the clerk certified the vote. That remains the central fact beneath all the cheering, the silence and the objections.

The people who got each other into the room

Jalen Mire was one of the people who had most to explain. His constituents helped win certification of a petition for the whole lake corridor to join Kelvant. That referendum was canceled after the competing whole-Territory petition qualified and the constitutional process required both claims to be considered together. Mire voted for a narrower departure because, he argued, a substantial northern part of the certified claim could now be put to a real future vote rather than lapse unanswered.

Tomas Rell voted for the same text because Korda's southern shore, service relationships and route toward Statehood would not simply be sacrificed to a referendum in which inland residents originally would have had no say. He did not promise that dividing obligations between jurisdictions would be easy. The resolution expressly leaves unresolved costs and titles open to lawful implementation rather than inventing a settlement in a single paragraph.

Dessa Orin voted yes, though she continues to prefer whole-Territory Statehood. She had forced the draft's central bargain to become enforceable conditions: lawful responsibility for services must continue during any transfer, and the Monitors must assess the actual territory seeking admission as a State. She told supporters outside the chamber that approving a proposal was not the same as approving the eventual geographical change. That judgment now belongs elsewhere.

Five votes against are not one objection

In the northern corridor, some petition organizers say a three-district proposal leaves southern petitioners without the same opportunity they helped certify. In interior Korda, opponents of any departure say the retained shore cannot compensate for losing freight and revenue. Others see the opposite risk: an implementing agreement might protect existing services but fail to produce the new investment the remaining Territory needs.

The resolution carries a fixed district schedule, tied to the Elections Panel's original petition-certification boundaries, not the committee's service geography. The precise authenticated schedule must be presented with any referendum materials. Its legal clarity will matter just as much to those who oppose it as to those who endorse it.

Speaker Elin Thoss congratulated the Convention on reaching a decision. She did not call the proposal her achievement or claim that other institutions were bound to approve it. Delegates who had spent long hours with her in private conversations credited no single speech for the margin; several spoke instead of the importance of knowing that the opposite side's conditions had been written down.

What happens after twenty-eight

The Elections Panel must administer the affected-voter ratification required by §15.5.a. The merger portion remains subject to §15.4's sixty-percent approval threshold and fifty-percent participation requirement. Kelvant must approve through its own constitutional process, the Legislative and Judicial Monitors must make the required integrity assessment, and the Senate must ratify by simple majority. No referendum result or approval is being reported today; none has happened.

Neither does the Convention vote itself admit the remaining Korda Territory as a State. The Monitors must complete the Statehood Audit requirements under §15.2. The constitutional clock that required the Convention to reach a proposed resolution has served its purpose; it does not transform the proposed arrangement into present law.

Korda's Convention began because two groups of citizens met different constitutional conditions for claims that could not proceed as though the other did not exist. It ends this phase with a choice broad enough for twenty-eight delegates to send to the people and difficult enough that the people's answer remains uncertain.

The chamber has decided what question to ask. Korda and the institutions whose consent the Constitution requires must now decide how to answer it.
