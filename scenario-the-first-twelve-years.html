Torenthia · Transition & Ratification · Article XIX

The First Twelve Years

Three sovereign countries decide to become one Republic. The ratification vote creates the new constitutional order in a day. Making that order work takes a generation.

§19.1 Ratification §19.2 Transition §19.3 Caretakers §19.5 Pre-Existing Obligations §19.6 Transition Territories §19.7 Candidate Registers §19.9 Required Institutions §19.10 Founding Classes

Six months before Day Zero, there is no Federated Republic.

There are three neighboring sovereign countries that have spent decades making it easier to live beside one another: the Harren Union in the north, the Torenthian Commonwealth across the western and central districts, and the Vardan Confederacy around much of Lake Varda and the southeast. Their borders do not match the State map that will exist later. Their institutions are different. Their debts are different. Their courts, ministries, military chains of command, pension systems, commercial rules, and diplomatic obligations are different.

They are also increasingly difficult to operate separately.

A manufacturer can sell across all three markets, but the same shipment can still encounter three customs administrations. Rail and energy infrastructure crosses borders that emergency planners no longer regard as meaningful. The Toren watershed requires joint management with Caldris, yet the northern neighbor can negotiate separately with three governments. Lake Varda puts Vardan and Commonwealth authorities on the same water facing the same increasingly unstable Sunderland government. Defense planning has reached the point where three militaries can cooperate faster than their political systems can authorize them to.

No country is collapsing. None has been conquered. None needs rescue.

The proposition placed before their governments is simpler and harder: they can continue coordinating sovereign governments indefinitely, or they can pool sovereignty and operate one.

Valedon, to the south, participates in the talks. It sees many of the same advantages and helps negotiate the regional settlement. In the end it chooses not to enter. It will remain close enough that Torenthians later call it the nation that almost joined.

The other three decide to proceed.

They do not convene a constitutional drafting convention. The constitutional framework they intend to use is already before them. Their work is narrower and more practical: a Treaty of Union has to identify the ratifying sovereigns, adapt the transition machinery to a simultaneous multi-country merger as §19.1 permits, allocate responsibility for Phase 0, and describe how the predecessor governments hand their legal obligations into the Republic without creating a day on which nobody is responsible for anything.

The Constitution tells them what the new government is. The Treaty of Union has to get them there.

Six Months Before Day Zero: The Merger File

Mira Venn chairs the Commonwealth transition secretariat because nobody else wants the job.

Her office occupies two floors of an old revenue building in Verentum. By Month -6 the walls are covered with lists.

Military commands. Courts. Ministries. Pension funds. State-owned companies. Treaty obligations. Property registries. Tax debts. Procurement contracts. Criminal cases already in progress. Civil cases waiting for judgment. Border stations. Diplomatic missions. Public hospitals. University grants. Classified archives. Election rolls.

Every item has to answer the same question:

What happens to this at 00:00 on Day Zero?

Tomas Arven, Vardan's chief transition negotiator, complains that the list makes union look like an accounting exercise.

“It is an accounting exercise,” Mira says. “The flags are the easy part.”

The Treaty of Union establishes that each of the three sovereign electorates must independently satisfy the constitutional ratification floor: at least sixty percent of votes cast, with at least fifty percent participation. No country can be carried into the Republic by the combined vote of the others. The interim Elections Panel will certify each result through the common National Voting System and, only when all three qualify, issue the single certification that creates Day Zero.

The three governments also agree that predecessor debts and obligations will be assumed by the Republic under the transition rules rather than extinguished by merger. Creditors are told there will be no clean slate. Pensioners are told their payments continue. Treaty partners receive notice that the Republic will succeed to obligations subject to the constitutional process for renegotiating anything incompatible with the new order.

The legal work is immense precisely because the political decision is simple.

They are not forming an alliance.

They are becoming one country.

Phase 0: Build the Things Ratification Assumes

The Constitution does not permit the countries to vote first and improvise election infrastructure afterward.

Before ratification, the interim Elections Panel must exist. The National Voting System must be operational and capable of meeting §11.2. A joint Monitor panel must be constituted from the pre-qualified transition pool.

So Phase 0 begins while all three predecessor governments are still fully sovereign.

Phase 0 · Pre-ratification preparation
The Republic does not exist yet. Its transition machinery does.

The interim Elections Panel builds the common ratification roll and verifies that voters in all three countries can use the NVS. The transitional Monitor pool is publicly qualified. The joint Monitor panel is constituted in advance so that an oversight vacuum does not open at ratification.

Sela Oran joins the technical transition staff from Calden. She spends most of Month -4 on something nobody puts in campaign speeches: record migration.

The predecessor governments hold millions of official records in incompatible systems. The Constitution says public records belong to the Republic once the transition begins and gives the NRS a permanent constitutional role. It does not make three incompatible databases become compatible by declaration.

Sela's team therefore divides the problem in two.

First, preserve. Nothing is discarded because it looks obsolete, duplicative, embarrassing, or inconvenient.

Second, authenticate. The Republic must be able to establish which predecessor government created a record, when it was created, whether it was later corrected, and which version was legally operative at Day Zero.

The transition staff does not decide whether an old policy was wise. It decides whether the future Republic can prove that the policy existed.

That distinction becomes the culture of the project.

Ratification Day

The campaigns are noisy and mostly familiar.

Supporters talk about one market, one external security policy, greater bargaining power with Caldris and Marisvia, and the ability to plan infrastructure without treating old sovereign borders as permanent obstacles.

Opponents ask whether the efficiencies are worth transferring powers that will be difficult to recover. Harren voters worry about population weight in the Assembly. Commonwealth voters worry that the Senate will over-represent smaller States. Vardan voters worry that decisions made in Verentum will treat Lake Varda as a distant regional problem.

Valedon watches from outside. Its government has concluded that the arrangement asks it to pool more sovereignty than it is prepared to pool. The decision is accepted without turning Valedon into an adversary.

Voting runs through the NVS.

The Harren result qualifies.

The Commonwealth result qualifies.

For nearly three hours, everyone waits on Vardan.

Then its participation floor is certified. The vote clears sixty percent.

The interim Elections Panel verifies the three results, signs the common certification, and publishes it.

Day Zero

The Federated Republic exists.

There is no interval between the predecessor countries and the Republic in which sovereignty is unassigned. The certification is the line.

At 23:59 there are three sovereign governments.

At 00:00 there is one Republic and a large number of caretaker institutions that look almost exactly like yesterday's governments.

That is the first surprise for people who expected federation to be visually dramatic.

The same bus drivers report to work. The same hospitals open. The same pension deposits arrive. The same police stations answer the telephone. The same judges have hearings scheduled.

But their legal environment has changed underneath them.

Ratification makes the constitutional obligations binding immediately. Laws conflicting with the Individual Sovereignty Floor are void from Day Zero. Predecessor officials are now caretakers. The twenty-four-month Transition Window starts running. Pre-existing social insurance and service obligations continue. The Republic assumes the predecessor international and financial obligations through the adapted union transition.

The new country is constitutionally real before its permanent institutions are complete.

Days 1–90: The Caretaker Problem

Phase 1 · Emergency Stabilization
Keep everything running without letting temporary governments make the permanent settlement.

Existing officials remain in place as caretakers. Social continuity comes first. New permanent legislation is barred. The joint Monitor panel watches the line between maintaining government and trying to govern beyond the caretaker mandate.

The first major argument arrives on Day 11.

A Commonwealth ministry has spent eighteen months developing a new national freight licensing regime. The regulation is complete. Industry expects it. Civil servants insist that delaying it will cost money and create confusion.

The minister signs the implementation order.

The joint Monitor panel publishes a finding the next morning: the order is not maintenance of an existing service. It creates a new permanent regulatory structure during the caretaker period.

The ministry argues that years of predecessor work should not be wasted because ratification happened to occur before the effective date.

The answer is unsympathetic and precise.

The work has not been erased. The authority to make it permanent now belongs to the institutions the new Constitution assigns that power to.

The order is withdrawn.

It becomes the first widely understood lesson of transition: continuity does not mean the old governments continue governing as though nothing happened.

The Laws That Vanish

The harder problem is the opposite one.

Some predecessor laws cannot continue even temporarily.

Article I operates from Day Zero. A statute inconsistent with the Individual Sovereignty Floor does not survive merely because the permanent federal Legislature has not met yet.

On Day 19, a Harren resident challenges a predecessor surveillance authority that permits administrative interception without the prior independent judicial authorization now required by §1.8.

The permanent federal courts are not fully constituted.

That does not eliminate the claim.

The temporary judicial mechanism required by §19.5(6) hears it.

The government argues for a transition period.

The court asks where the Constitution grants one.

There is none.

The conflicting authority is void.

For the transition staff, that ruling changes the workload overnight. Every predecessor institution has been asking which old laws will be replaced later. Now they understand that some already have been.

The Classified Inheritance

Three intelligence systems become one constitutional problem.

Some classified material is obviously legitimate: source identities, active operational details, military capabilities. Other files were classified under predecessor rules that allowed secrecy for reasons Article X no longer recognizes.

No mass declassification occurs on Day Zero. No blanket preservation occurs either.

The records are now subject to the constitutional standards. Temporary review machinery operates until the permanent independent mechanism can be established after Phase 4.

The result irritates everyone.

Security officials think review is too aggressive.

Transparency advocates think it is too slow.

Sela considers that evidence the mechanism may be located approximately where it should be.

The Founding Indigenous Register

Before Phase 1 ends, the joint Monitor panel compiles the founding register of recognized Indigenous nations from the predecessor records.

This is not a power to decide which peoples deserve sovereignty. §16.1 makes the function ministerial: the panel identifies nations already carrying the treaty, compact, or formal recognition required by the predecessor record.

Korda appears on the register.

The Republic then has a constitutional duty to do something predecessor governments often avoided: ask what relationship the nation itself chooses.

Korda does not have to answer immediately.

The choice remains open through the Transition Window.

That time matters.

Days 91–270: Turn the Map Into Institutions

Phase 2 · Institutional Construction
The country exists. Now its permanent constitutional architecture has to exist too.

Statehood Audits proceed, the permanent Monitors are constituted, the NVS reaches full deployment, and the transition begins converting inherited jurisdictions into the Republic's State/Territory structure.

The Treaty of Union did not simply declare that the three predecessor countries would become three giant States.

Their internal political units already vary too much for that. Some hold recognized State-equivalent status and choose direct founding State entry under §19.6(6). Some predecessor territories enter as Territories. Others use the transition procedures available to them.

The emerging map is therefore a constitutional map, not a recoloring of the old international borders.

Harren enters as a founding State and carries enough of the old country's institutional identity that people continue to describe it as a founding State twelve years later. Varek, Arvane, Rhovane, Merath, Orath, Kelvant, Caldenmere, Aldenmere, Selvane and other jurisdictions settle into the State structure through their predecessor statuses and transition choices. Verdmont, Morantine and Solara enter the territorial structure. Corindal's later difficulties will demonstrate that founding membership never exempts a State from the continuing audit rules.

Korda takes longer.

Its own institutions conduct the status process required by §16.2. Federal transition officers are repeatedly reminded that they may explain the options but may not prefer one.

The debate inside Korda is not framed as “join or leave.” Kordans are already living through the creation of the Republic around them. The choice is the constitutional relationship they want to hold with it.

Associated Community status would preserve a fully sovereign compact relationship.

Territorial Integration would place Korda inside the Republic as a Territory, with the ordinary Statehood pathway available later.

Korda elects Territorial Integration.

The decision is permanent as to the founding Compact option. Nobody at the time knows that, more than a decade later, Korda will be arguing fiercely over what kind of Statehood future it wants.

The Monitors Stop Being Temporary

The Legislative, Executive, and Judicial Monitors are constituted during Phase 2.

The shift looks small on paper and enormous to the transition staff.

Until now, a joint interim panel has been watching institutions that are themselves temporary. Now each permanent Monitor begins acquiring its own staff, procedures, candidate mechanisms, protected funding, and constitutional identity.

The first Monitor Generals quickly discover the limit of their authority.

They can investigate.

They can verify.

They can publish.

They usually cannot command.

Ressa Korr, the first Legislative Monitor General, tells his staff on their first morning: “If you want to run the Legislature, resign and stand for it. Our job is to make it impossible for them to say nobody knew.”

The sentence stays on the LM training wall long after Korr leaves office.

Days 271–450: The First Election Is Not About Ratification

Phase 3 · First Elections
The coalition that created the Republic immediately stops being a governing coalition.

Assembly elections, Senate formation, the first Legat Consul election, judicial selection, and the founding constitutional offices move from transition administration into democratic competition.

The ratification campaign had made politics look simpler than it was.

People who agreed that the Republic should exist did not agree about taxation, welfare, environmental regulation, defense spending, the size of ministries, infrastructure, immigration, labor law, or how much policy should remain with the States.

The first federal election releases all of those arguments at once.

There is no founding party.

There are four substantial Assembly blocs, several smaller groupings, and a large number of members who ran locally on combinations nobody in Verentum predicted.

The first Senate is seated from the States. The Elections Panel conducts the public lot required by §19.10. One cohort draws two-year initial terms, one four, one six, establishing the stagger without pretending anyone was elected for a term the Constitution did not assign.

Edran Vale, an old Harren union advocate, draws a two-year term.

He laughs when the result is announced.

“That is what public lots are for,” he tells reporters. “They prevent me from arguing I deserved six.”

The first Supreme Court classes are staggered by the same principle. Candidate registers assembled by the transition panel carry forward until the permanent machinery can take them over.

The Legat Consul election produces a clear winner: Anja Serin, a former Vardan foreign minister who campaigned on the proposition that the Republic's external voice must become operational quickly enough that Caldris and Sunderland stop testing which predecessor capital still matters.

Then the Assembly tries to choose a Civic Consul.

And cannot.

The First Government Does Not Form

For twenty-one days the new Assembly votes, negotiates, adjourns, meets again, and discovers that winning the argument for a Republic was easier than assembling an absolute majority for a domestic government.

No bloc is close enough.

On Day 18, two blocs produce a joint platform and still fall two votes short.

On Day 21, they try again.

Same result.

At the deadline, nobody has to decide whether the situation counts as a constitutional crisis.

§2.6.a has already decided what it counts as.

The Assembly Speaker becomes Acting Civic Consul by constitutional operation.

The Speaker keeps the Assembly seat. Authority is restricted to maintaining existing policy. The acting service is published to the NRS. The caretaker cannot use the ordinary suspensive veto and cannot turn temporary possession of the office into political advantage.

The Assembly conducts the nominating ballot.

Four candidates advance.

The Elections Panel schedules the national ranked-choice election.

For citizens who had spent nearly two years hearing that transition required patience, this is an irritating development. They had been promised the permanent government.

What they receive first is another temporary one.

But the lights stay on.

The NRS keeps publishing.

The Legat Consul exercises the external authorities of the office.

The courts continue sitting.

The Assembly continues legislating within the rules governing an acting Civic Consul.

The government is incomplete, but there is no vacancy to seize.

The national vote finally produces a majority for Leva Coren, an Assembly member from Selvane who had entered the formation process as a compromise candidate and discovered that being everyone's second choice can be enough in a ranked-choice election.

When the Elections Panel certifies the result, Leva becomes Civic Consul with full authority and vacates her Assembly seat.

The acting service ends immediately.

No transfer ceremony creates the authority. The certification establishes who won; the Constitution supplies what follows.

The first full government of the Republic is now in place.

The transition is not yet over.

Phase 4: Full Constitutional Operation

The first Legislature convenes with the executives constitutionally installed, and Phase 4 begins.

Now the constitutional deadlines that were waiting for a permanent government begin to matter.

The civic proficiency assessment administrator must be established within the constitutional maximum.

The Monetary Authority must become constitutionally capable.

The transition from predecessor election infrastructure to the permanent NVS must be legislated without creating a gap in democratic participation.

The independent review system for predecessor classified material has its own deadline.

The Legislature has a first budget to write for a government assembled from institutions that six months earlier belonged to three different sovereign countries.

Mira Venn's transition secretariat begins closing files.

Departments that once sent her questions now send them to permanent ministries.

Temporary mechanisms terminate when permanent ones become constitutionally capable.

One afternoon she realizes that this is the first week in two years when nobody has asked her who is responsible for something.

She considers that the clearest sign the transition is working.

Year 2

The First Ordinary Budget

The first budget assembled entirely under permanent institutions is ugly.

Three predecessor tax systems have left different expectations about what belongs in federal revenue and what should remain with States. Ministries submit duplicate requests for functions that have not yet been fully consolidated. States complain that transitional grants have become invisible baselines. The Monetary Authority rejects one set of growth assumptions as inconsistent with its independent projections.

The argument lasts months.

That is precisely what makes it important.

Nobody seriously proposes giving the Civic Consul authority to settle the budget alone because the new government is too young for legislative disagreement. Nobody proposes suspending State powers until the fiscal map is cleaner. Nobody claims transition requires another year of caretaker government.

The Republic has reached the stage where constitutional conflict is becoming ordinary conflict.

The budget passes.

It is amended seven months later.

No history book commemorates either vote.

Year 4

One Government Meets Caldris

The Toren River supplies one of the reasons the three predecessor countries believed a single external government might be useful.

Now the proposition is tested.

Caldris controls the main-stem headwaters. An eastern tributary rises in the Torenthian Alps, runs north into Caldris, and supplies much of the dry-season flow. The hydrology makes simple claims about upstream and downstream almost useless. Each side can hurt the other.

Before union, Caldris could negotiate different aspects of the watershed with different governments. Each agreement created another seam.

In Year 4, Legat Consul Anja Serin's successor opens negotiations on behalf of one Republic.

The Senate ratifies the resulting framework.

The agreement does not eliminate dispute. Twelve years later there will still be a dispute-resolution gap important enough to make news.

What changes is the identity of the counterparty.

Caldris no longer negotiates with Harren about one reach of the river and the Commonwealth about another.

It negotiates with Torenthia.

For the first time, one of the practical promises behind the merger is visible from outside the Republic.

Year 6

The Founders Stop Owning the Argument

By Year 6, the people who administered transition have begun to become historical figures while still being alive enough to complain about the histories.

Edran Vale leaves the Senate after later service and returns to Hadrin. Mira Venn teaches public administration twice a week and refuses every invitation to describe herself as a founder. Sela Oran remains in records work long enough to watch younger NRS engineers replace systems she once considered modern.

The important change is elsewhere.

Half the Assembly members elected that year did not participate in the ratification campaign.

They do not treat a disagreement with the Constitution as ingratitude to the generation that implemented it. They propose amendments. They litigate boundaries. They complain about the Senate. They accuse the Monitors of overreach and weakness, sometimes in the same week.

The Constitution is ceasing to be a transition document in public memory.

It is becoming the rules.

Year 8

A Government With No Transition Credentials

Casimir Rehn holds the Legat Consulship.

His claim to office has nothing to do with the merger. He did not run as the custodian of Day Zero. He ran on contemporary questions: regional security, trade, the Toren relationship, and the growing uncertainty east of Lake Varda.

He appoints Ines Carrow as Director of Foreign Affairs.

Carrow's staff contains civil servants who began their careers in predecessor ministries, people hired during transition, and younger officials for whom those categories mean almost nothing.

This mixture is visible everywhere.

The federal bureaucracy has stopped asking which predecessor institution a procedure came from unless there is a legal reason to care.

States have developed their own political identities inside the Republic.

The Senate's stagger is no longer described as a founding arrangement; it is simply the Senate cycle.

The initial judicial classes have turned into ordinary vacancies and appointments.

The first long constitutional terms are beginning to turn over.

A government generation is passing.

Years 9–11

The Problems Change

Sunderland deteriorates.

The change is gradual enough that no single record marks the moment it becomes a different kind of neighbor.

Authorities fracture. Messages become less reliable. Refugee movement across Lake Varda grows. Coast-guard planning becomes more serious. Intelligence that once supported routine diplomacy begins supporting contingency planning.

The Republic's institutions now encounter problems that had nothing to do with why the predecessor countries merged.

That is a more meaningful test than repeating the founding conditions forever.

The Legat Consul has the external portfolio but cannot convert every security concern into unilateral domestic authority.

The States remain responsible for their own constitutional domains even when federal policy would be easier if they did not.

The NRS preserves acts that officials would sometimes prefer the public encountered only through summaries.

Monitors publish findings that elected officials dispute.

Courts decide cases no transition planner anticipated.

The constitutional order is not proving that it can solve every problem.

It is proving that new problems do not require inventing a new government each time.

Year 12 · Month 8

The End of the First Generation

The Republic is twelve years old.

The longest founding institutional terms have now carried the system across roughly one full generation of government. Offices have changed hands. Judicial classes have turned. Senators drawn into short founding cohorts are long gone. Temporary transition institutions are memories in archived NRS records.

People who were children at ratification are voting, serving in government, and entering national politics.

Valedon remains outside and close.

Korda remains a Territory, now carrying arguments about statehood and internal geography that nobody on Day Zero could have resolved for it.

The Toren framework exists and remains imperfect.

Sunderland's disorder presses against Lake Varda.

Rehn's tenure is approaching its end.

Carrow is nationally known through Foreign Affairs. Seren Mak has built a political base in Rhovane. Tobias Vael has become a recognizable northern sovereignty voice. Pell Sandris is doing the slower work of building a candidacy around policy almost nobody expects to dominate the race.

None of them needs to explain why Harren, the Commonwealth, and Vardan merged.

They are not governing a merger.

They are governing the Republic.

Year 12 · Month 9

A candidacy filing enters the National Record System.

The Elections Panel certifies Tobias Vael as eligible to stand for Legat Consul.

The filing is routine.

That is what twelve years of transition and government have produced: a constitutional event significant enough to reshape national politics and ordinary enough to arrive as another record in a system that nobody needs to invent that morning.

From this point, Torenthia's public record takes over.

Constitutional finding

Article XIX makes ratification an immediate change in sovereign authority and transition a managed change in institutions. The predecessor governments do not disappear before something can replace them; they become limited caretakers while permanent federal and State institutions are constructed around constitutional deadlines.

The test is not whether merger is painless. It is whether ordinary government, rights, obligations, elections, courts, records, and public services remain constitutionally accountable while three sovereign systems become one. Twelve years later, the strongest evidence of success is that Torenthia's politics are no longer mainly about transition.
