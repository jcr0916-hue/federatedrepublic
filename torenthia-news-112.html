Korda · The Settlement

The List Before the Vote

After a Convention decision and a boundary authentication, the difficult next question is not what is being proposed. It is who must be allowed to decide.

Mara IsetKorda Press·Year 14, Month 1

The Northern Lake Settlement is specific about its geography. The Korda Convention proposed transferring three northern lake-corridor districts to Kelvant, leaving the southern shore and interior with Korda. The Elections Panel has already authenticated LC-N1, LC-N2 and LC-N3 against the original certified boundaries. A line can now be described without pretending it has moved.

But a boundary on a schedule is not an electorate. The Panel's new docket notice begins the work of verifying who belongs on the affected-voter roll for the settlement decision. It requests reconciliation of residence registers and a means of correcting disputed entries. It does not publish a final list or announce when a vote will take place.

The difference between a map and a mandate

A map can be compared with an earlier map. A voter list requires judgments about records made by offices at different times, people who moved, and errors that may not become visible until someone finds they have been left out. Every correction procedure has a consequence: an accurate roll is necessary for a meaningful vote, but endless revision could become its own form of delay.

Some advocates of the northern settlement want the Panel to move quickly, arguing that the Convention has already spent enough time on the question. Others will want every contested entry resolved before any ballot date is fixed. Both demands can be made in good faith. Neither allows an official to treat an unverified roll as certified merely because the politics has become impatient.

What the Convention did not decide

The Convention's 28–5 resolution proposed a settlement; it did not approve a merger by itself. An affected-voter ratification remains to be held. Kelvant's constitutional approval, the applicable §15.4 requirements, the joint Legislative–Judicial Monitor integrity assessment and Senate ratification remain distinct steps. Korda's possible Statehood likewise belongs to its own process, not to this electorate docket.

That sequence is cumbersome only if one assumes a Convention ought to be able to speak for everyone whose status it would change. The Constitution assigns different decisions to different bodies because agreement in one room is not the same as consent from everyone legally entitled to give it.

For Korda's residents, the next consequential document may appear dull: a notice describing who qualifies for the ballot and how to challenge an omission. No border will change when it is published. But it will determine whether the next argument is about the proposed settlement—or about whether the Republic allowed the right people to judge it.
