Judicial Independence · Removal for Cause · §4.3.a · §4.5

The Cause

Year 10. A Supreme Court justice is accused of serious undisclosed financial misconduct days after authoring a bitterly unpopular constitutional ruling. The evidence may justify removal. The politics may not.

§4.3.a Supreme Court Justice Removal§4.5 Judicial Review and Scope§7.10 Ethics and Financial Disclosure§9.1 Monitor Assessments

Associate Justice Mara Venn authored the Court's 5–4 decision striking down the National Port Security Act. The ruling was lawful, narrow, and politically explosive. Within a week, legislative leaders from both governing parties called Venn reckless, unelected, and dangerous.

Three days later, an investigative disclosure produced a different problem. Venn had held a substantial beneficial interest in a logistics partnership while hearing two cases whose outcomes materially affected the partnership's federal contracts. The interest had not appeared on her required financial disclosure. Internal correspondence suggested she knew of the connection before oral argument and declined to recuse.

Two Allegations, Only One Constitutional Ground

§4.3.a — Judicial disagreement is never cause
A legislature may despise a judgment. It may not convert that disagreement into a removal ground.

Several senators filed a resolution describing the Port Security decision as evidence that Venn had "abandoned the constitutional order." That language did not create cause. §4.3.a makes judicial disagreement — however profound — constitutionally insufficient.

The undisclosed financial interest was different. If established, deliberate nondisclosure and participation in matters touching that interest could constitute serious misconduct and potentially constitutional breach in the exercise of judicial authority. The same political actors could therefore be motivated by an impermissible reason while relying on facts that might independently support a permissible ground.

The Monitor Assessment

The Assembly requested a Monitor assessment. The assessment concluded that the disclosure failure was substantiated, that the financial interest was material, and that the justice had received written notice of the conflict before the second case. It expressly declined to evaluate the correctness of Venn's Port Security opinion.

§4.3.a — Informative, not constitutive
The Monitor can develop the record. It does not decide whether a justice is removed.

The assessment was published to the NRS. It was not a prerequisite to removal and did not itself suspend Venn. Removal still required stated constitutional grounds and concurrent two-thirds votes of both chambers.

The Election Window

The proposed removal vote was scheduled 142 days before the next major national election. That timing activated the additional safeguard in §4.3.a. Before either chamber could hold the removal vote, the Supreme Court had to review the proposed proceeding under §4.5 and rule within forty-eight hours.

Venn recused from that review. The remaining participating justices did not decide whether the chambers should remove her. They decided whether the published grounds were constitutionally cognizable and whether the proceeding could lawfully occur inside the protected election window.

The Court's narrow answer
Political hostility does not immunize real misconduct, but real misconduct does not legalize a political purge.

The Court struck the portions of the resolution that treated the Port Security judgment itself as a removal ground. It allowed the proceeding to continue on the separately stated allegations of deliberate nondisclosure, failure to recuse, and serious misconduct.

The Assembly then voted 78% for removal. The Senate voted 65% — one vote short of two-thirds of its full seated membership. Venn remained a justice. No lesser vote, Monitor finding, or public anger could substitute for the constitutional threshold.

What This Scenario Tests

§4.3.a protects judicial independence without making Supreme Court justices untouchable. The Constitution distinguishes sharply between disagreement with a judicial decision and misconduct by the person who made it. The former can never be removal cause; the latter can be.

The election-period review is an anti-purge safeguard, not a transfer of the removal decision to the Court. The Court polices the constitutional grounds and procedure. The political branches still make the ultimate removal decision, and they must each reach two-thirds.
