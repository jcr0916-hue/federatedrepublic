The distance between a harvest and a market

For three seasons, drivers at Dorven’s grain co-op kept their own tallies against weigh tickets they no longer trusted. When the board finally replaced the freight scale, a technician came from Merath to calibrate it. Corwin Hale, editor of The Dorven Ledger, thought the delay deserved more attention than the machinery. In the Republic’s agricultural heartland, independence still depends on things people have to maintain together. (The Co-op Finally Fixed the Scale.)

Orath occupies the eastern-central interior. Dorven gives its agricultural economy a familiar public voice: grain prices, planting credit, freight capacity, and a weekly paper willing to ask who is responsible when ordinary arrangements stop working. The fields explain much of the State’s identity. Its Constitution takes the next step, turning cooperation, access to productive resources, and the cost of governing dispersed communities into institutions.

That inheritance leaves room for change. The preamble remembers farmers, market-keepers, and families following the rivers inland, while expressly contemplating a State whose work changes across generations. Agriculture and cooperative traditions inform the constitutional design without giving any occupation a permanent constitutional privilege. (Preamble; State §13.6.)

The Grange chooses a Steward

Orath’s single legislative chamber is formally the Orath General Assembly. Popular speech calls it the Grange, preserving the cooperative movement’s place in political memory. The Constitution allows between seventy-five and 105 members, with the actual size set by statute. Approximately two-thirds represent geographic districts; approximately one-third come through a statewide proportional or compensatory tier. Members serve four-year terms. The design gives local communities an identifiable voice while requiring substantial proportionality across the State. (State §§2.1–2.3.)

At the start of its term, the Assembly selects a sitting member as Steward of Orath. The selected member must meet the office’s citizenship requirements and immediately vacates the legislative seat. The Steward serves the remainder of the Assembly term and may be elected to one additional regular term. Political removal requires a constructive vote of no confidence: a majority of authorized Assembly membership must elect a named successor in the same vote. Responsibility for choosing a government stays visible in the chamber. (State §§3.2–3.3.)

The Steward administers State law, proposes the budget, and can return legislation with written objections. A veto can be overridden by two-thirds of authorized Assembly membership. Emergency declarations require a factual basis; a unilateral declaration expires after fourteen days, with legislative extensions limited to thirty days apiece. Labor disruption alone is expressly insufficient grounds for an emergency. (State §§3.4–3.6.)

The county and the cooperative

Orath’s local life rests on two different kinds of association. Counties and municipalities are constitutionally protected territorial governments. Every unincorporated area belongs to a county, with directly elected county representation. Rural roads, emergency response, election logistics, and other territorial services can be administered there under law. Incorporation is not a prerequisite for competent public services. (State §§5.1–5.3, §5.9.)

A cooperative governs shared economic life: its equipment, property, enterprises, leases, and resources lawfully under its control. Its charter gives it no general territorial authority over nonmembers. That distinction matters wherever a grain elevator, shared machinery, or cooperative land shapes a community’s working day. Public roads, elections, policing, and general taxation remain responsibilities of public government. (State §5.4.)

Cooperation also carries obligations toward the people doing the work. A qualifying worker can petition for fair consideration of cooperative membership and receive written reasons for denial; membership is not automatic. Labor Arbitration Tribunals hear employment and cooperative-labor disputes, with written decisions and appeal to the appropriate District Court. Labor protections extend to all workers in Orath, and an employer cannot use sponsorship or immigration threats to punish someone for asserting them. (State §§1.5–1.6, §4.4, §6.4.)

Water has more than one claimant

The Constitution places water alongside livelihood and local government among Orath’s defining concerns. Its right to reasonable access covers households, communities, and lawful enterprises. Scarcity brings priorities: human consumption and sanitation, ecological and public-health minimums, essential community needs, and essential productive use. It promises reasonable basic access without assigning everyone a fixed quantity. (State §1.4.)

The Toren Tributary Water Board is charged with allocation and long-term water-rights administration within its lawful jurisdiction. Upstream and downstream regions and the major categories of affected users must be fairly represented; no category may control the Board. Transfers cannot extinguish reasonable basic community access or defeat protected downstream rights and ecological minimums. Interstate disputes follow lawful compacts and federal procedures. These are rules for sharing a resource across competing needs, with the detailed choices left to lawful administration. (State §10.2.)

The price of serving a long road

Population alone is an incomplete measure of what local government costs in Orath. State equalization transfers must account for the actual cost of required services and may consider road miles, service distance, density, emergency-response costs, and local tax capacity. Low population cannot by itself justify depriving a community of reasonable essential services. A statewide capital plan covers roads, bridges, communications, water systems, and emergency access. (State §§9.6–9.7.)

Education makes that commitment concrete. The State retains ultimate responsibility for access despite remoteness or weak local finances. Transportation or another effective means of participation must make attendance practicable. Remote instruction can help, but cannot simply become a cheaper route to persistently inferior education for remote students. Local accountability and regional cooperation both have a place in the system. (State §§11.1–11.4.)

The same concern for continuity shapes finance and public records. An Orath Stabilization Reserve Fund can support communities through drought, crop failure, market collapse, major employer failure, or other severe shocks. The Orath State Record uses the State’s section of the National Record System, with access through county offices, libraries, printable copies, and other means for people without reliable private connectivity. A constitutional right must still be usable at the far end of the road. (State §9.5; Article VIII.)

Dorven’s next practical question

In Year 14, Month 1, Dorven’s freight administrators asked grain handlers and carriers for updated seasonal shipment estimates. Their coordination notice separates projected loading capacity from confirmed shipments. It declares no shortage, imposes no quota, and announces no federal intervention. The work is ordinary, and it still has to be done.

Hale’s latest Ledger report uses that notice to ask what national candidates understand about transport responsibilities. The approaching spring Legat Consul election supplies the occasion; it supplies no answers on their behalf. For Orath, the public question begins with the next load of grain: which decisions belong to carriers and cooperatives, which require county or State action, and where the Republic’s responsibility actually starts.
