Four Answers, One Correct

This paper put the same question to all four Legat Consul candidates: what did Wednesday's Executive Monitor certification actually find? The answers ranged from broadly right to substantially wrong, and the ranking has almost nothing to do with which ones will travel furthest.

Corwin Hale The Dorven Ledger · Dorven Year 13, Month 9

Readers of this paper have a direct stake in the answer. Orath is not one of the advantaged states, and it is not one of the territories at the back of the queue either. It sits in the part of the distribution where the formula works well enough that nobody argues about it, which is a comfortable place to be right up until the moment the formula changes.

So the question mattered here, and we asked it plainly: did the Executive Monitor find that the equalization mechanism has failed?

Seren Mak

"The Monitor's own numbers show a system that has doubled the wait for the territories in four years. Call that whatever you want to call it. I call it a failure, and I think most people reading those numbers will call it a failure too."

This is the answer with the most road ahead of it, and it is not what the certification says. The Monitor found no failure — explicitly, in a single sentence, which is why the ninety-day legislative response clock under §12.8 has not started. Mak is describing the supplementary data and calling it the finding. Those are different documents in the same file.

Ines Carrow

"The certification found no operational failure, and I'm not going to say otherwise for applause. What it found is that the standard we're certifying against may not be the standard we want. That's a legislative question, and I'd note it's one the Legislature has had the power to answer for eleven years without needing anyone's permission."

Accurate, and characteristically pointed at the end. Carrow gets the finding right and then uses it to make an argument about legislative inaction, which is a fair use of a correct premise.

Tobias Vael

"I read it twice. It says the machine is doing what we told it to do. That's not the same as saying we told it the right thing. I don't have a slogan for that."

Vael, as usual, declines to make it easier to quote than it deserves to be. He has the substance right. He also has, by our count, given the shortest answer of any candidate to any question this campaign.

Pell Sandris

"No. The Monitor certified that the mechanism is operating as the statute defines it, which is the only question §12.8 asks it to answer annually. No failure was found, so no legislative response is required. The interval data published alongside it is new, and it is significant, but it is context, not a finding — and the Legislature has to decide for itself what to do with context. That decision is not automatic, and I'd rather it not be. A mechanism that forces a response every time someone finds the outcomes upsetting is a mechanism that hands whoever defines 'upsetting' an enormous amount of power."

That is the correct answer, complete, and it took him ninety-one words to give it. It will not appear on a single broadcast this week.

What This Paper Thinks Readers Should Take From It

Three of the four candidates understand what the certification says. Two of those three said so plainly. One built a better applause line by describing the data as the finding, and that line will outrun all the accurate ones, because it is shorter and angrier and requires nothing of the listener.

We are not going to pretend that is a scandal. It is an ordinary feature of elections, and Mak is not lying — she is describing real numbers that describe a real problem. But readers in Orath deciding how to vote on a formula that currently works for them should know the difference between "the mechanism failed" and "the mechanism did exactly what we built it to do, and we may not like what we built." The second one is harder to say and considerably harder to fix.
