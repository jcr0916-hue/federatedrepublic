The Second Declaration

Year 3. A 14-day regional emergency expires. Seventy-two hours later, a new emergency issues — same geography, same rights restrictions, different label. The mandatory 48-hour judicial review applies the 60-day bar. The SC rules on two violations at once.

§1.16 Emergency Derogation §1.16.a Non-Derogable Rights §4.5 Judicial Review and Scope

The Civic Consul's first emergency declaration covered the Holta Region, where sustained civil unrest following disputed local elections had disrupted infrastructure and public safety. The declaration was narrowly scoped — geographic limits specified, rights restrictions enumerated (freedom of movement and assembly), duration set at 14 days, NRS publication on Day 1. It ran its course and lapsed automatically on Day 14. No continuing statute was enacted.

Seventy-two hours later, a second declaration issued. This one was titled "Regional Infrastructure Security Emergency." It covered the same geographic boundaries as the first. It restricted the same rights — freedom of movement, assembly, and access to certain public facilities. The stated basis was different: not the unrest itself, but the degraded infrastructure that the unrest had left behind. The Civic Consul's office published it to the NRS immediately as required. The 48-hour judicial review clock started the same day.

The Relabeling Bar

§1.16 — The 60-Day Bar
A new label does not reset the constitutional clock. A substantially similar derogation may not be declared within 60 days after expiry.

§1.16 bars a substantially similar derogation for 60 days after a declaration expires; after that, re-declaration requires 2/3 of both chambers. The test is substance: substantially the same scope, substantially the same rights restricted. The stated rationale is not the test — the bar cannot be evaded by changing the description of why the emergency exists.

Every declaration receives independent judicial review within 48 hours of publication, so the question reaches a court at once. The second declaration issued on Day 17, three days after the first lapsed, well inside the 60-day bar, and covered the same geographic boundaries and the same rights as the first.

The Discrimination Finding

§1.16.a — Emergency Measures Applied Unequally
The SC hears two violations simultaneously. The second emerged from the evidence.

Three civil liberties organizations and a coalition of Holta Region residents petitioned the SC the day the second declaration was published. The petition raised the relabeling bar violation. But the evidentiary submissions included something the petition had noted only peripherally: enforcement of the movement restrictions had been significantly more intensive in the two predominantly Hareni-heritage districts within the declaration zone than in comparable adjacent districts.

§1.16.a is absolute. No emergency measure may be applied in a discriminatory manner on the basis of race, ethnicity, religion, national origin, sexual orientation, or gender identity. Any such application is constitutionally void from the first instance of unequal application — not from the date of a court ruling, not from the date of formal challenge. The court ruling confirms and remedies what is already void.

The SC's Ruling

The SC ruled on Day 19, within the 48-hour review period. Two violations; one ruling.

On the relabeling violation: the second declaration was void as a relabeled continuation of the first under §1.16. The 60-day bar had not expired when the second declaration issued (Day 17, three days after the first lapsed on Day 14). All measures taken under the second declaration were void from issuance. The CC was notified immediately. If a genuine infrastructure emergency required emergency response, the path forward was a new declaration after the 60-day bar expired — and that re-declaration would require 2/3 of both chambers.

On the discrimination violation: movement restrictions had been applied unequally in the Hareni-heritage districts under both the first and second declarations. Both applications were separately void. The CC's office was required to publish a remediation plan within 30 days specifying how the enforcement disparity would be addressed, what redress was available to those whose movement had been unlawfully restricted, and what monitoring framework would prevent recurrence.

The scenario tests what the relabeling bar is actually for. It is not a technicality — it is the constitution's answer to the risk that emergency powers, once used, create institutional appetite for continued use. The 60-day cooling period forces genuine reconsideration. The mandatory 48-hour judicial review forces prompt scrutiny. The SC's jurisdiction over emergency rights challenges during an emergency — explicitly preserved under §4.5 — ensures that the cooling period cannot be evaded by relabeling what is substantively the same emergency. A new label does not create a new emergency.
