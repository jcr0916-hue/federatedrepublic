The Man Who Filed the Requests

For nine years, Halden Rews ran the office that sent Korda's capacity requests into the federal queue. They stayed there. He is now the minister responsible for territorial infrastructure, and the stretch of Korda he used to administer has filed to leave the Territory altogether. He would like people to consider that these are the same story.

Petra Vend The Torenthian · Verentum Year 13, Month 9

Halden Rews has been a minister for three weeks and has said almost nothing publicly in that time, which his staff attribute to his being genuinely busy and his critics attribute to his being a careful man in a government under pressure. Both may be true. When he finally sat down with this paper on Tuesday, he came with a specific thing he wanted said, and he said it early.

"I filed capacity requests for the lake corridor for nine years. Every one of them was properly documented. Every one of them went into the queue. Most of them are still there. I am now the minister responsible for territorial infrastructure, and last week the corridor petitioned to leave Korda. I don't think those are separate news items."

The petition — certified last week by the Elections Panel — carries the signatures of a quarter of the lake corridor's eligible voters, enough to trigger the process that could eventually place the question of merging with Kelvant before them in a referendum. No vote has been held. Under §15.5.a, none can be until the JMC completes an assessment of whether what remains of Korda could stand on its own, a process that may run 90 days or longer.

Rews was careful, repeatedly and visibly, not to comment on whether the petition should succeed.

"That's not my call and it shouldn't be. Those are their signatures, not mine. What I'll say is that people don't file that kind of petition when things are working. They file it when they've done the arithmetic on how long the other route takes."

On the Argument He Is Not Making

Asked directly whether the Civic Consul was right to open the §12.8 review without an Executive Monitor finding — the objection Senator Aldous Fenn raised last week and repeated at the joint committee's first session — Rews declined, twice, and on the second occasion explained why.

"Senator Fenn is asking a real question about process. I'm a minister. If I start telling the Legislature which procedural objections are worth taking seriously, I've made his point for him. What I can tell you is what the four years cost, because I was the one filing during them. That's the part I actually know."

What it cost, by his account: two rebuilds of the corridor's northern approach that were scoped, costed, and never funded; a coast guard station operating on temporary authorization since Year 10; and a pattern he described in unusually plain terms for a sitting minister.

"Every year I filed, and every year the answer was that the mechanism hadn't identified a shortfall. The mechanism was looking at whether the formula was working as designed. It was. That was the problem. It was working exactly as designed and the corridor still couldn't get a bridge rebuilt."

The Part He Would Not Say Cleanly

Twice during the conversation, Rews stopped short of finishing a thought about what the corridor's departure would mean for the rest of Korda — the inland districts, older and poorer, that would remain behind. Asked a third time, he answered slowly.

"I grew up inland. Not in the corridor. My family is still there." A pause. "I understand the arithmetic the corridor did. I understand it better than most people in this building. I'd just rather they hadn't been in a position where the arithmetic came out that way."

He would not be drawn further, and the interview moved to the committee's request for an Executive Monitor assessment, which he called "the right thing to have asked for, whenever it arrives."
