Two Names on the Paper, Thirty-One Still to Answer

The committee's two co-chairs have finally signed the same political document. The third name many delegates want to see is not on it.

Mara IsetThe Korda Press·KordaYear 13, Month 12

The first thing Jalen Mire and Tomas Rell did after filing their joint memorandum was insist that it was not a bargain.

They were correct in a technical sense. The filing contains no final boundary, no cost allocation, no recommendation by the Joint Committee on Transition Facts and no vote of the Territory Convention. The two men signed as delegates, not as committee co-chairs. Nothing in it can transfer a district to Kelvant or make the remainder of Korda a State.

But the signatures mattered because Mire and Rell began on opposite sides of the only question that had seemed able to organize the room. Mire defended the certified lake-corridor petition and its canceled referendum. Rell defended the interior against an exit that might take too much of Korda's economic capacity and lake access. Their new paper asks whether a smaller northern departure, rather than the entire certified corridor, could answer one claim without making the other impossible.

It is the first common text of that kind either has put his name to.

Orin has questions, not a signature

Dessa Orin did not sign. She was not asked to pretend that the Statehood petition's supporters had abandoned their original objective. In remarks after the memorandum circulated, she distinguished a useful drafting exercise from a resolution she could recommend to her constituents.

The distinction rested on more than political pride. A line that leaves Korda a southern lakeshore but strips out the personnel, facilities or recurring support that make that shore usable would not satisfy her. Nor would a plan that describes the remaining Territory's future only as an application to be considered someday. The Statehood Audit process already exists, she noted; any Convention resolution must state honestly what survives of that process and what still has to be demonstrated.

She also asked whose accounts would pay for bridges, shared facilities and outstanding contracts after the jurisdictional change. The Joint Committee's preliminary compilation lists gaps in precisely those areas. She would not accept numbers the evidence did not support just to make a compromise look complete.

Those demands expose a difficult fact for supporters of a narrower merger. Saving the southern lakefront on paper may be politically necessary. It does not, by itself, save the services that join that shore to interior communities.

A vote count with no count announced

Some delegates sympathetic to the corridor say Mire must explain why residents of districts still inside the certified petition area should accept a less extensive departure than the one for which they gathered signatures. Others say the Constitution brought conflicting claims together because residents outside that proposed departure would bear consequences without having participated in the original corridor vote.

The legislature's own delegates are now testing whether both concerns can be addressed by a boundary schedule that separates northern service connections from southern lake obligations, coupled with enforceable continuity arrangements. The memorandum asks for those schedules before a final resolution is voted on. It does not say who would prepare them, how disputed title would be adjudicated or how Kelvant would approve the eventual merger.

Assembly Speaker Elin Thoss has been seen returning to the same small groups of delegates more than once. People who attended those conversations describe questions about conditions for support rather than a speech for any particular map. None would say that Thoss had collected a majority, and nobody has filed a vote tally on the proposal because there is no proposal ready for such a vote.

What happens if agreement fails

The constitutional consequence remains plain. The Convention's ninety active days did not stop when the committee started gathering evidence and did not restart when the memorandum was filed. If the Convention cannot produce a resolution in its allotted period, the competing petitions lapse together and Korda remains undivided, with the restrictions on reinitiating substantially identical geographic petitions that §15.5.a prescribes.

If the delegates do produce a resolution, that is only the next constitutional step. Affected voters must still ratify what the Convention proposes, and the merger and Statehood pathways retain their respective legal requirements.

For now the chamber is doing what the committee was never empowered to do: arguing about obligations, rights and political geography rather than merely compiling evidence. Mire and Rell have put two names on a page. Whether Orin and enough others can accept what follows is the unanswered question that gives the page its weight.
