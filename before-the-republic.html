Before the Republic

Three countries decide that security and prosperity may require something larger than cooperation. A fourth comes close. What follows is the story of the arguments that became the Federated Republic.

Reading note. This is Torenthian canon, told as a long-form prequel. It begins two years before ratification and ends where the published World record begins in Year 12, Month 9. Present-day State boundaries did not map neatly onto the predecessor countries; the union created a new constitutional order rather than simply renaming three old ones.
Part I · Year -2, Month 1

Three Countries

The first document that would eventually create the Federated Republic did not contain the word constitution.

It contained a table.

Four columns. Thirty-seven rows. One page of explanatory notes underneath.

The columns were headed Harren Union, Torenthian Commonwealth, Vardan Confederacy, and Valedon.

The rows listed things governments usually discussed separately: customs rates, rail gauges, military procurement, port duties, passport recognition, agricultural inspection, river access, electrical interconnection, mutual disaster assistance.

At the bottom, someone had written by hand:

There is no authority competent to resolve disagreement between the parties.

Mira Venn read the sentence twice.

Then she looked across the conference table.

“Who wrote this?”

Nobody answered.

The room belonged to the Harren Ministry of Commerce, though the Harren delegation had spent the morning insisting that this did not make Harren the host. Four national flags stood behind the long table. Four sets of stenographers sat against the wall. Four foreign ministries had spent six weeks negotiating what the meeting would be called.

Not a summit. Not a conference of union. Not constitutional talks.

The official name was the Western Continental Coordination Meeting on Trade, Infrastructure, and Collective Security.

It had taken twenty-three words to avoid frightening anybody.

Mira set down the page.

“Whoever wrote it,” she said, “they are correct.”

Across from her, Edran Vale of Harren stopped turning his pencil between his fingers. He was sixty-two, silver-haired, and had spent most of his adult life explaining why Harren did not require anyone else’s permission to govern itself.

Beside him, Tomas Arven of the Vardan Confederacy gave a short laugh.

“That is an ambitious thing for a Commonwealth official to say on the first morning.”

“I run the office that tells our ministries when they have contradicted one another.”

“Then you are a civil servant with enemies.”

“That is fair.”

At the far end of the table, Valedon’s Foreign Minister, Ilyan Seret, tapped the handwritten sentence.

“There is no authority because we have not created one.”

“Correct.”

“And I assume the Commonwealth position is that we now should.”

“The Commonwealth position is that the river does not care about the Commonwealth position.”

That silenced the room.

The Toren crossed borders long before the diplomats did. Agreements governing it had accumulated in layers over nearly forty years. Some were bilateral. Some were emergency protocols. Some had expired but were still followed because nobody wanted to discover what happened if they stopped.

The system worked most of the time.

That was the problem. Everything they had come to discuss worked most of the time.

Trade moved. Trains crossed. Hospitals treated people from neighboring countries and sent invoices afterward. Fire crews crossed borders before diplomats could authorize them. None of this was collapse. It was simply expensive, repetitive, and fragile.

Edran Vale leaned back.

“So what are you proposing?”

“Nothing yet.”

“You brought us a page saying our governments are structurally incompetent.”

“I brought you a page saying our governments are competent in four different directions.”

Year -2 · Month 3

The second meeting was held in Verentum. By then the newspapers had discovered the first one.

Publicly, all four governments denied discussing political union.

Privately, three had commissioned papers on it.

Harren’s began with the question: What powers must never be transferred?

The Commonwealth’s began: What functions can no longer be efficiently exercised separately?

Vardan’s consisted mostly of red lines.

No permanent federal police. No unilateral alteration of internal boundaries. No national executive with plenary authority. No abolition of constituent legislatures.

At the bottom someone had written:

If these conditions make union impossible, union is impossible.

Mira liked that paper best.

It sounded like negotiations rather than aspiration.

The person who first gave the future NRS its shape was not a minister. Sela Oran was twenty-nine, a municipal records officer from Calden, invited because a working group needed someone who understood how many legal identities a freight company could possess at once.

During a dispute over cross-border permits, she produced three authentic government records describing the same warehouse authorization. Two contained different load limits.

“Three official records,” she said. “All authentic. All produced lawfully. Two different facts.”

Someone suggested better information-sharing.

Sela shook her head.

“If you create an authority whose acts bind people in all of these countries, there has to be one place where the authoritative act exists. Not a press release. Not a ministry copy. Not whatever version somebody happened to receive. One record.”

“A federal gazette.”

“No. A gazette publishes what the government wants published. I mean the record of what the government did.”

By the end of the afternoon the working group had filled twelve pages.

At the bottom of the twelfth was a sentence that would survive every later draft:

No institution should control the record of its own legality.
Year -2 · Month 7

The security meeting took place in Rhondel, overlooking Lake Varda.

The immediate proposal was modest: common procurement standards, shared emergency communications, and a standing command protocol if an armed incident affected more than one country.

Everyone supported the first two.

The third nearly ended the talks.

“Whose command?” Tomas Arven asked.

Harren suggested rotation. The Commonwealth said rotation was unsuitable during an emergency. Valedon proposed a joint council. Arven asked what happened if the council disagreed while ships were already moving.

Edran Vale finally asked the question they had avoided for seven months.

“Why are we designing a military command before deciding whether there is a country?”

Mira looked out toward Sunderland.

“Because everyone in this room knows what happens if somebody fires across that water.”

The answer they reached that week was not a complete constitution. It was a sketch: one external executive for defense, diplomacy, and intelligence; a separate domestic executive accountable to a legislature; neither entitled to absorb the other’s field simply because a crisis made concentration convenient.

Arven remained suspicious.

“The domestic executive would need to be removable.”

“By the legislature,” Mira said.

“Without leaving the country without a government.”

“Then removal must install the replacement at the same time.”

Edran wrote that down.

Year -2 · Month 11

By the end of the year the question was no longer whether they were designing a new country. The question was whether they could admit it publicly.

Edran Vale did so in Hadrin.

We are discussing whether several sovereign countries can create a new sovereign republic together.

He told the audience that the question was not what Harren would surrender, but what authority it would choose to exercise with others—and what it would refuse to surrender at all.

Then he said the sentence that became the moral center of the convention:

If we build this, we should build a government that does not require good people in order to remain safe.

In Valedon, Ilyan Seret turned off the broadcast and opened a memorandum that had sat unopened on his desk for nine days.

Its title was Conditions Under Which Valedon Could Enter a Federated Republic.

Part II · Year -1, Month 1

The Convention

The invitation finally used the word openly.

Convention for the Formation of a Federated Republic.

Harren accepted. The Commonwealth accepted. Vardan accepted.

Valedon took eleven days and added one sentence to its reply:

Participation in the Convention constitutes no commitment to ratification.

“Good,” Mira said when she read it.

Her deputy looked surprised.

“If they were already committed, there would be no point holding a convention.”

The delegations arrived in Verentum with incompatible ideas of what union meant. Harren wanted a federation whose central powers could be listed on one page. Commonwealth delegates wanted enough common authority to make the common market real. Vardan demanded that no federal institution be able to erase a member polity by ordinary law. Valedon wanted proof that a federation would remain voluntary in spirit even after becoming binding in law.

And then the convention discovered that the four flags did not represent everyone whose future it was discussing.

The Fifth Chair

Ilyan Orre arrived from Korda on the third morning.

He was not part of the Vardan delegation.

This distinction mattered greatly to him and somewhat less to Vardan.

Korda’s relationship with the Vardan Confederacy was old, negotiated, periodically resentful, and difficult to explain in a room organized around national flags. Some powers were Vardan. Some were Kordan. Some rested on compacts older than either constitution in the room.

The organizers had solved this by inviting Vardan.

Korda solved it by coming anyway.

A spare chair stood against the wall.

“Is that ours?” Orre asked.

The protocol officer hesitated. “It was intended for territorial observers.”

“Which territories?”

No answer.

Orre pulled the chair to the table.

“Then today it is Korda’s.”

Later histories made this sound heroic. In reality someone had to find him a microphone and lunch had been ordered for the wrong number of people.

History often improved itself in memory.

The actual beginning was usually administrative.

When discussion turned to the proposed common defense authority, Orre raised his hand.

“If you expect Korda to live under this government, I would like to know whether Vardan will agree to it for us or whether we will agree to it ourselves.”

That question did more than win Korda a seat. It forced the Convention to distinguish between constituent governments and sovereignty that predated them.

By Month 4, the working draft no longer described Indigenous authority as delegated local government. It said that prior sovereignty was acknowledged, not granted.

Several delegates objected that the Constitution was recognizing something it could not control.

Orre answered, “That is what recognition means.”

The Two Chambers

The Assembly was easy to imagine and hard to agree upon. Representation by population had democratic clarity. Harren and Vardan both feared that the largest population centers would dominate it. Equal representation protected constituent communities but would let smaller populations outvote larger ones.

The compromise became two chambers with different jobs.

The Assembly would be the direct political chamber: population-based, close to ordinary government, capable of choosing and replacing the Civic Consul.

The Senate would represent the States as States and serve as the harder checking body: treaties, confirmations, veto overrides, and institutional verification.

“You are building inefficiency on purpose,” a Commonwealth newspaper complained.

Edran clipped the editorial and carried it in his folder for the rest of the Convention.

Whenever someone proposed removing a check because it was inconvenient, he placed the clipping on the table.

“Yes,” he would say. “Some inefficiency is the product.”

The Rights Floor

The fiercest argument was not about institutions.

It was about whether there were things no majority should be able to do.

Valedon’s delegation preferred a shorter rights declaration and broader legislative discretion. Harren’s constitutionalists wanted enforceable rights with direct judicial remedy. Vardan delegates insisted that emergency powers could not become a side door around the bargain.

Mira’s position was simpler.

“If the Constitution says a person has a right only when government is comfortable granting it, the Constitution has described a policy.”

The resulting Article I was longer than anyone wanted.

That became part of its point.

The rights were written as operational floors, with courts available to enforce them and a set of protections that no emergency could suspend.

Ilyan Seret warned that detailed rights language would constrain future governments in circumstances the founders could not anticipate.

“Yes,” Mira said.

“You say that as though it settles the objection.”

“It is the answer to it.”

The Monitors

Sela Oran’s common record had by then acquired a name: the National Record System.

The problem that followed was obvious.

If every constitutional act went into a permanent record, who would tell the public whether the institutions filling that record were actually following the Constitution?

Not the executive, because it would be grading itself.

Not the Legislature, for the same reason.

Not the courts alone, because courts answered cases and controversies, not every procedural failure.

The Convention tried inspectors with enforcement powers. It tried auditors answerable to the chambers. It tried one general oversight office.

Every version created a new institution powerful enough to become a problem of its own.

The eventual answer was deliberately weaker: three independent Monitors, each watching a different branch, publishing facts and compliance findings but generally unable to command the institution being watched.

Tomas Arven hated it at first.

“Then they can be ignored.”

“Politically, perhaps,” Sela said.

“Then what is the point?”

“You wanted an oversight institution that could not quietly become a fourth branch of government. This is what that costs.”

“Information is not power.”

Sela looked at him.

“Then you have never watched a government explain a permanent public record during an election.”

Part III · Year -1, Months 6–10

The Country That Almost Joined

Valedon did not drift away from the Convention.

It moved closer.

That was what made the eventual refusal hurt.

Seret negotiated seriously. Valedon’s lawyers improved the treaty clauses. Its election specialists helped design the rules preventing an executive from postponing a vote. Its provincial representatives supported the Senate because they understood the fear of population centers overwhelming smaller communities.

For a while, the draft assumed four ratifying countries.

The break came over something deeper than a clause.

Valedon’s government believed sovereignty could be pooled for particular purposes while remaining ultimately recoverable by the national institutions that had pooled it.

The other three increasingly believed the new Republic had to become a sovereign order in its own right. States could retain enormous constitutional power. Communities could leave through lawful processes. Indigenous nations could preserve or choose sovereign relationships. But the federal government could not function as a committee whose members could disregard it whenever domestic politics changed.

Seret understood the distinction perfectly.

He simply did not agree.

In Month 8, he and Mira walked the sea road after a fourteen-hour drafting session.

“You think we are afraid,” he said.

“I think you are careful.”

“Diplomatic answer.”

“Fine. I think you want the benefits of permanence without accepting that permanence limits the people who come after you.”

Seret laughed softly.

“And you want to bind people not yet born because you distrust people not yet elected.”

“Exactly.”

They stopped at the seawall.

“That,” Seret said, “may be the most honest argument for a constitution I have heard here.”

Valedon stayed through the final draft.

Its delegation signed the Convention record certifying that the text had been produced lawfully.

It did not sign the Treaty of Union.

Seret’s final address was not angry.

We believe this Republic may become a good neighbor. We are not persuaded that it should become our government.

Three delegations stood to applaud.

The fourth chair remained at the table until the Convention adjourned.

Part IV · Year -1, Month 11 to Year 0

Ratification

The final text was published before any government could summarize it for its own voters.

That was Sela’s victory.

The draft Constitution, the Treaty of Union, the Convention votes, dissents, technical annexes, and corrections entered the transitional record together. Governments could campaign for ratification. They could criticize. They could explain. They could not quietly substitute a cleaner version of what had happened.

The Treaty of Union adapted Article XIX for multiple sovereign countries pooling sovereignty simultaneously. It did not erase their obligations. Public debts remained debts. Pensions remained pensions. Treaties remained treaties until lawfully superseded. Predecessor institutions would become caretakers during transition rather than vanish overnight.

The campaign lasted months.

In Harren, skeptics warned that the Assembly would eventually consume State authority.

In the Commonwealth, opponents said the Senate gave too much power to smaller States.

In Vardan, critics called the Legat Consul a disguised presidency and the NRS an administrative impossibility.

Kordan debates centered on something else: whether participation in the new order would preserve the ability of Indigenous peoples to decide their own relationship to it.

The Constitution’s answer was deliberately not one answer.

Recognized Indigenous nations would enter the transition with sovereignty acknowledged rather than created. Each would choose its status through its own lawful process. Associated Community. Territorial integration. Where independently qualified, a founding State path. No federal official was permitted to prefer one choice.

Ilyan Orre called that “the first provision I have seen in a national constitution that understands not everyone dreams of becoming a subdivision.”

Day Zero

The ratification vote was not held because the founders were certain it would pass.

It was held because the Constitution required a public answer large enough to bind the new order.

When the interim Elections Panel certified the required threshold, the Republic existed.

There was no coronation. No transfer of a crown. No single official whose signature made the country real.

The certification entered the record.

Day Zero began.

Conflicting predecessor laws against the Individual Sovereignty Floor became void. Public assets transferred into the constitutional record framework. Existing officials became caretakers. The twenty-four-month Transition Window opened.

At 09:14, Sela Oran searched the new National Record System for the certification she had watched being filed.

It was there.

She searched from a second terminal.

Still there.

Then she laughed so hard the technician beside her thought something had failed.

Part V · Years 0–2

Building the Republic

The Republic existed before it was ready.

Article XIX had been written for exactly that uncomfortable fact.

For the first ninety days, predecessor officials kept services running as caretakers. They could maintain; they could not use transition as an excuse to make permanent policy.

The joint Monitor panel began publishing findings before the permanent Monitors existed. Temporary courts heard rights claims because the absence of a finished judiciary could not suspend the right to remedy. Old classified files entered review under standards their authors had never expected to face.

The first political fights were not glamorous.

Which payroll system survived long enough to pay federal employees?

Which predecessor procurement contracts transferred?

How were overlapping agencies separated without losing records?

Which places entered as States on the strength of recognized predecessor status, which entered as Territories, and which chose a different sovereign relationship?

Present-day borders emerged from that transition and its later constitutional processes. They were not a simple tracing of Harren, Commonwealth, and Vardan lines onto a new map.

By Phase 3, the temporary country began becoming a political one.

Assembly candidates campaigned across new districts. States chose senators. The first Legat Consul election forced voters to think nationally about foreign affairs for the first time. Judicial candidates entered the new pool. The first permanent Monitors were selected by institutions deliberately prevented from appointing the officials who would watch them.

Mira Venn did not run for anything.

Neither did Sela Oran.

Edran Vale returned to Harren and won a Senate seat, then drew one of the short founding terms in the public lot.

Tomas Arven became one of Vardan’s most persistent critics of the government he had helped design.

“I helped write the brakes,” he told a reporter. “Someone should check whether they work.”

The first Assembly

The general election returned four large blocs and no governing majority.

For twenty-one days, the Assembly tried to elect a Civic Consul.

It failed.

No army moved.

No outgoing minister declared necessity.

No court invented a government.

At the end of the statutory window, the Assembly Speaker became Acting Civic Consul by constitutional operation.

Nothing about the appointment was discretionary.

The Speaker retained the Assembly seat and gained only caretaker authority: maintain existing policy, keep administration functioning, do not use the suspensive veto, do not pretend temporary service is a mandate.

The acting service appeared on the NRS immediately.

The Assembly then held the nominating ballot required by the Constitution. Four candidates advanced.

The Elections Panel scheduled the national vote.

For the first time since ratification, the founders were forced to watch a mechanism they had argued about in theory operate against the interests of real political actors.

One coalition tried to close the gap before the national election began. It came within two Assembly votes and failed.

After that, the choice left the chamber.

Millions of voters ranked the candidates.

The winner emerged with a majority after preferences were redistributed and became Civic Consul with full authority.

The Speaker’s acting service ended at that instant.

Sela read the certification from Calden.

Mira read it in Verentum.

Edran read it from his Senate office and sent Mira a note containing six words:

The people, once asked, are not un-asked.

Mira kept it.

The Republic had survived its first government-formation deadlock not by finding a better politician, but by refusing to lower the legitimacy threshold.

That mattered more to her than who had won.

Part VI · Years 4–11

After the Founders

Constitutions become ordinary slowly.

In Year 4, the Toren River forced the Republic to negotiate with Caldris as one country rather than three. The resulting framework did not solve every future dispute. It did something more basic: it proved that the external executive and Senate could carry a coherent national position into a relationship that had once been divided among predecessor governments.

Edran Vale called Mira after the agreement was published.

“We have one counterparty now,” he said.

“You sound disappointed.”

“I am looking for the hidden cost.”

“Good.”

“You people made suspicion into a civic virtue.”

“No. We made it less dangerous.”

By Year 8, the names of the founding negotiators appeared less often in newspapers. That was another success.

Casimir Rehn held the Legat Consulship. Ines Carrow worked in Foreign Affairs. New Assembly members arrived who had been children during the Convention. State governments fought with the federal government over money, implementation, and competence without treating every dispute as a referendum on whether the Republic should exist.

The NRS became boring.

Sela considered that her greatest achievement.

People complained about records being late, over-redacted, badly formatted, or embarrassingly detailed. They rarely questioned whether a constitutional act would leave a record at all.

The Monitors were accused, depending on the month, of being toothless, intrusive, pedantic, political, timid, and impossible to ignore.

Tomas Arven sent Sela a clipping containing all six accusations.

Across the eastern border, Sunderland became less predictable.

What had once been difficult diplomacy began to acquire the vocabulary of instability. Authorities changed. Messages went unanswered. Lake Varda became not simply a shared body of water but a route by which another country’s disorder could reach the Republic.

The Constitution did not prevent any of that.

It told Torenthians who could act, who had to authorize, what had to be recorded, and where citizens could challenge the answer.

That was all it had ever promised.

Part VII · Year 12

The World Catches Up

By Year 12, nobody under thirty could remember living in Harren, the Commonwealth, or Vardan as sovereign countries.

The predecessor names survived in archives, family stories, old institutional habits, and arguments about which founding country had really invented which part of the Republic.

Valedon remained outside.

Close enough to be familiar. Independent enough to make the counterfactual unavoidable.

Korda remained constitutionally distinct, carrying forward questions about territory, sovereignty, infrastructure, and belonging that the Convention had refused to settle by pretending they were all the same problem.

Rehn’s tenure was nearing its end.

Carrow had become a recognizable national figure through Foreign Affairs. Seren Mak was making herself difficult to ignore from Rhovane. Tobias Vael had developed a northern audience around sovereignty and external pressure. Pell Sandris was building a candidacy almost nobody treated seriously enough.

Lake Varda was becoming more dangerous.

The Toren relationship with Caldris still carried unfinished business.

The Republic was not in a founding moment anymore.

That was why what came next mattered.

Year 12 · Month 9

On an ordinary morning, a candidacy filing entered the National Record System.

The document was valid. The Elections Panel accepted it. The permanent record assigned it a number.

Tobias Vael was running for Legat Consul.

No one in the filing mentioned Mira Venn.

No one mentioned Edran Vale, Tomas Arven, Sela Oran, Ilyan Seret, or the fifth chair.

No one needed to.

The institutions they had argued into existence were now old enough to belong to people who had not built them.

That was the point.

The prequel ends. The public record begins.
