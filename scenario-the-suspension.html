LM Monitor General · Removal · §9.9

The Suspension

Year 8. Both executives file removal grounds against the Legislative Monitor General. She keeps her authority while the filing is pending. The question goes to the public — and the clock starts running against the people who filed it.

§9.9 Constitutional Officer Removal §9.7.a Institutional Compromise Protocol §7.3 Electoral Supermajority Threshold §9.2 Selection and Independence

The Legislative Monitor General audits the Legislature. That is the whole of her mandate — she observes what the chambers do, assesses compliance, and publishes findings to the National Record System. She cannot compel anyone. Her findings bind no one. She has, in eight years, published four findings that a sitting Legislature would have preferred unpublished.

In Year 8 she publishes a fifth: a compliance finding that the Assembly's budget-origination process has, across three consecutive cycles, been used to move appropriations outside the ordinary committee record. The finding names no one. It recommends nothing. It is, by constitutional design, information and nothing more.

Eleven days later, the Civic Consul and the Legat Consul jointly file stated grounds for her removal — abuse of office, they allege, in how she framed the finding. The Constitution allows removing a Monitor General for serious misconduct, abuse of office, or permanent incapacity. Disagreeing with her finding would not be a ground.

Who Is Allowed to File

The first thing the Constitution decides is not whether the grounds are good. It is who may raise them at all.

§9.2 states the principle the entire Monitor system rests on: each Monitor is constituted through a process that structurally excludes the body being watched from selecting its watchers. The Supreme Court nominates the Legislative Monitor General. The two chamber Speakers jointly nominate the Executive Monitor General. The Judicial Monitor General is drawn by public lottery. Nobody picks the person who audits them.

§9.9 applies the same rule at the other end. Removal of a Monitor General is initiated by the branch or branches that Monitor does not audit. The Legislative Monitor General audits the Legislature — so the Legislature cannot move against her. The executives can, and here they have.

§9.9(1) — Who Initiates

For the Legislative Monitor General, either Consul may initiate. For the Executive Monitor General, either chamber may initiate. For the Judicial Monitor General, either chamber or either Consul may initiate. Joint action is permissible here, but it is not required.

No branch participates in removing the officer who audits it.

This is not a formality. The finding that provoked the filing concerned the Assembly. The Assembly has no route to act on it. Members can be furious in public and can say so; they cannot file. The executives can — and the question of whether they filed on the Legislature's behalf is exactly the question the public will be asked to weigh.

What Filing Actually Does

Nothing changes that afternoon. She keeps her office, her staff, and her authority, and she keeps publishing. The Constitution does not let an accusation do the work of a verdict: filing puts the grounds on the National Record System and starts the clock, and that is all it does.

Filing alone cannot switch off oversight. Under §9.7.a the other two Monitor Generals may publish findings recommending suspension; approval must then come from the institution constitutionally specified for that Monitor. For the LM, a majority of the Supreme Court must approve. A valid suspension lasts at most ninety days unless removal proceedings have begun; continuation then remains subject to expedited review of necessity. Temporary incapacity follows §9.1.e, and permanent incapacity has the separate safeguards in §9.9(4). No suspension or incapacity is established here.

§9.9(1) — Filing Is Not Suspension

The initiating authority files stated grounds to the NRS. Filing does not suspend the Monitor General's authority.

Her salary and her ten-year clock are untouched; nothing in the filing reaches them. She is an officer under accusation, not an officer discharged, and the accusation itself carries no penalty. An officer serving a fixed, non-renewable term, watching the bodies that could file against her, is precisely the person for whom a penalty imposed on filing would work best. The Constitution does not offer one.

Who Decides

Not the executives who filed. Not the Legislature she audited. Not the Supreme Court.

For the misconduct allegation here, §9.9(2) requires a national removal referendum: 60% of votes cast, without a turnout requirement. Permanent incapacity is a distinct route, requiring a recommendation by the other two Monitor Generals, two-thirds of each chamber and both Consuls. The executives cannot convert this disagreement over a finding into either route merely by describing it differently.

The referendum is scheduled for Day 74. The stated grounds are on the National Record System, where anyone can read them. So is the finding that preceded them by eleven days. Both documents are dated. The public can see the sequence without anyone having to characterize it.

The Clock Runs Against the Filer

The design's sharpest feature is what happens if nothing happens.

§9.9(2)–(3) — The Deadline and the Continuing Duty

A referendum is administered no later than 90 days from filing, or at the next regularly scheduled federal election, whichever comes first.

Failure to conduct the referendum within the required period does not terminate the proceeding. The Elections Panel remains obliged to administer it. The Monitor retains authority unless separately and lawfully suspended.

Filing starts an enforceable referendum duty. It does not remove or suspend the Monitor. If the vote is late, the filing remains unresolved and judicial remedies remain available; administrative inaction supplies neither a public verdict nor an indefinite suspension.

The safeguard is continuity of oversight while the public decision remains outstanding. A missed deadline cannot manufacture removal, and a pending accusation cannot take the place of the separate suspension process. The Day 74 vote in this case meets the deadline, so no judicial enforcement of that duty is needed.

What the Public Decided

On Day 74 the referendum fails. Participation is 61%. Removal draws 38% of votes cast, well short of the 60% required.

The proceeding closes that day. She never lost her authority, so there is nothing to restore: she goes on exactly as before. The compliance finding that started all of this remains exactly where it was published, unaltered, because nothing in the removal process ever reached it.

§9.9(3) bars re-initiation on the same or substantially similar grounds arising from the same conduct for the remainder of her term — two years — while preserving proceedings based on materially new conduct or evidence. A failed national referendum is a stronger verdict than a failed legislative vote, and permitting a second attempt every six months would let an initiating branch wear down an officer it could not remove.

What This Demonstrates

An oversight officer who cannot be removed is unaccountable. An oversight officer who can be removed by the people she oversees is not an oversight officer at all. §9.9 refuses both, and it does so by separating three questions most systems collapse into one.

Who may raise the question — only the initiating authorities specified for that Monitor in §9.9. What raising it accomplishes — a public statement of grounds and a vote, and nothing else; not suspension, not pay, not term, not the published record. Who answers it — the same public that confirmed her, by the sixty-percent threshold this section requires.

The executives were entitled to file. The public was entitled to disagree. Both things happened, in the open, on a record with dates on it. That is the whole mechanism working — not the accusation failing, but the accusation being made to go somewhere and be answered by someone with the standing to answer it.
