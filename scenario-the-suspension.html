LM Monitor General · Removal · §9.9

The Suspension

Year 8. Both executives file removal grounds against the Legislative Monitor General. Her authority ends that afternoon. Her salary does not. The question goes to the public — and the clock starts running against the people who filed it.

§9.9 Constitutional Officer Removal §9.1.d Acting Mechanism §7.3 Electoral Supermajority §9.2 Selection and Independence

The Legislative Monitor General audits the Legislature. That is the whole of her mandate — she observes what the chambers do, verifies compliance, and publishes findings to the National Record System. She cannot compel anyone. Her findings bind no one. She has, in eight years, published four findings that a sitting Legislature would have preferred unpublished.

In Year 8 she publishes a fifth: a compliance finding that the Assembly's budget-origination process has, across three consecutive cycles, been used to move appropriations outside the ordinary committee record. The finding names no one. It recommends nothing. It is, by constitutional design, information and nothing more.

Eleven days later, the Civic Consul and the Legat Consul jointly file stated grounds for her removal.

Who Is Allowed to File

The first thing the Constitution decides is not whether the grounds are good. It is who may raise them at all.

§9.2 states the principle the entire Monitor system rests on: each Monitor is constituted through a process that structurally excludes the body being watched from selecting its watchers. The Supreme Court nominates the Legislative Monitor General. The two chamber Speakers jointly nominate the Executive Monitor General. The Judicial Monitor General is drawn by public lottery. Nobody picks the person who audits them.

§9.9 applies the same rule at the other end. Removal of a Monitor General is initiated by the branch or branches that Monitor does not audit. The Legislative Monitor General audits the Legislature — so the Legislature cannot move against her. The executives can, and here they have.

§9.9(2) — Who Initiates

For the Legislative Monitor General, jointly by both executives. For the Executive Monitor General, by the Supreme Court. For the Judicial Monitor General, jointly by the Legislature and both executives.

No branch participates in removing the officer who audits it.

This is not a formality. The finding that provoked the filing concerned the Assembly. The Assembly has no route to act on it. Members can be furious in public and can say so; they cannot file. The executives can — and the question of whether they filed on the Legislature's behalf is exactly the question the public will be asked to weigh.

What Filing Actually Does

Her constitutional authority stops the same afternoon. Not at a hearing, not on a finding of wrongdoing — on the filing itself. §9.9(3) suspends a Monitor General's authority immediately, and §9.1.d's acting mechanism activates from that moment, so the audit function does not go dark while the question is unresolved.

Three things do not stop.

§9.9(3) — Suspension Is Not Removal

Suspension does not affect the Monitor General's compensation, benefits, or the running of their term, all of which continue in full unless and until removal is certified.

Her salary continues. Her benefits continue. Her ten-year clock keeps running, so a suspension cannot be used to quietly extend the period before a successor is due. She is an officer under accusation, not an officer discharged — and the Constitution refuses to let the accusation itself function as a penalty. An officer serving a fixed, non-renewable term watching the bodies that could file against her is precisely the person for whom financial pressure would work best. The provision closes that door before anyone reaches for it.

Who Decides

Not the executives who filed. Not the Legislature she audited. Not the Supreme Court.

The Legislative Monitor General was confirmed into office by national popular vote. §9.9(4) requires that only a national referendum can end her term — 60% of votes cast under §7.3, with at least 55% citizen participation. The body that granted the office is the only body that can take it back.

The referendum is scheduled for Day 74. The stated grounds are on the National Record System, where anyone can read them. So is the finding that preceded them by eleven days. Both documents are dated. The public can see the sequence without anyone having to characterize it.

The Clock Runs Against the Filer

The design's sharpest feature is what happens if nothing happens.

§9.9(4)–(5) — The Deadline and the Default

A referendum is administered no later than 90 days from filing, or at the next regularly scheduled federal election, whichever comes first.

Where the referendum fails, or where no referendum is administered within that period, the suspension lapses automatically and the Monitor General resumes full authority by constitutional operation, with no further act required.

Filing suspends her. It does not suspend her indefinitely. If the executives file and then find that the public mood has turned — if they would now prefer no vote at all — declining to hold one does not leave her sidelined. It restores her. The suspension expires on its own, and nobody has to vote, rule, or petition to make that happen.

This inverts the usual incentive. In most systems, an accusation that goes nowhere still damages the accused, because the proceeding simply stalls and the cloud remains. Here, a proceeding that stalls ends in the accused officer's favor automatically. The filers must either put the question to the public or watch their own filing dissolve.

What the Public Decided

On Day 74 the referendum fails. Participation is 61%. Removal draws 38% of votes cast, well short of the 60% required.

The suspension lapses that day. She resumes full authority — not by grant, not by reinstatement, but by constitutional operation, the way an expired restriction simply ceases to restrict. The acting Monitor General returns to the pool under §9.1.d. The compliance finding that started all of this remains exactly where it was published, unaltered, because nothing in the removal process ever reached it.

§9.9(6) bars re-initiation on the same or substantially similar grounds for the remainder of her term — two years. A failed national referendum is a stronger verdict than a failed legislative vote, and permitting a second attempt every six months would let an initiating branch wear down an officer it could not remove.

What This Demonstrates

An oversight officer who cannot be removed is unaccountable. An oversight officer who can be removed by the people she oversees is not an oversight officer at all. §9.9 refuses both, and it does so by separating three questions most systems collapse into one.

Who may raise the question — anyone except the branch being audited. What raising it accomplishes — suspension of authority, and nothing else; not pay, not term, not the published record. Who answers it — the same public that confirmed her, at the same threshold that removes a Legat Consul.

The executives were entitled to file. The public was entitled to disagree. Both things happened, in the open, on a record with dates on it. That is the whole mechanism working — not the accusation failing, but the accusation being made to go somewhere and be answered by someone with the standing to answer it.
