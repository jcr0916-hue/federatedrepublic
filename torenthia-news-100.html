The Equalization Debate Has Reached the Formula

For months the argument was about whether the current system could see the burdens Territories were describing. The committee now has to decide what, if anything, the statute should count differently.

Dara Voss RNN Institutional Correspondent · Verentum Year 13, Month 12

The Joint Committee on Fiscal Equalization has not recommended changing the formula.

It has done something that makes that decision harder to avoid.

A comparative options memorandum filed with the National Record System puts three concrete approaches in front of members: keep the existing allocation formula while making request age easier to see; add a factor for recurring service burdens; or create a blended factor that can recognize verified infrastructure backlog together with requests that remain unresolved across repeated review periods.

None of those approaches is a committee position. There is no draft bill, no agreed weighting and no markup date. The Executive Monitor has not found the present mechanism constitutionally deficient, which means the ninety-day remedy process in §12.6 is still not running.

But the review has moved from a dispute over evidence to a dispute over design.

The first option changes the record, not the allocation

Option A is the least intrusive. The formula stays where it is. What changes is the public record around requests: standardized reporting would show how long a request has been pending, how often it has been deferred and what eventually happened to it.

That would answer one of the easier criticisms of the present system. A project that has waited through several review cycles would no longer disappear into the same category as something filed recently.

It would not, however, require the equalization mechanism to care that the older request is older.

That distinction is likely to matter in Morantine, whose submission emphasized request age and recurring delivery costs. The Territory could gain a clearer public record of delay without gaining any additional statutory priority from it.

The second option asks what governments have to keep doing

Option B would add recurring service burden as an explicit factor.

That sounds straightforward until someone has to write the definition.

A mountain road that costs more to maintain because of terrain is a recurring burden. So is a border service operating at unusually high volume. So may be a dispersed public-service network whose per-resident delivery cost is structurally higher than the national norm.

But every government can describe some ordinary expenditure as unusually difficult. A formula broad enough to recognize every claimed burden stops distinguishing among them. A formula narrow enough to avoid that problem can recreate the present complaint: measurable needs exist, but the statutory categories fail to see them.

The staff memorandum does not solve that problem. It names it.

The third option gets closest to the political grievance

Option C is the most direct response to the queue argument. It would let the mechanism consider a documented infrastructure backlog together with the persistence of qualifying requests across review periods.

Importantly, age by itself would not win. A request would still have to correspond to an actual capacity need and be measured against common standards. The oldest file would not automatically become the first file funded.

That construction addresses a fear raised by several State submissions: that turning waiting time into a decisive factor would allow a weak or obsolete request to become stronger merely by surviving long enough in the queue.

It also raises the hardest legislative question in the memorandum. If persistence matters, how much should it matter?

That is no longer an administrative question. It is a choice about what the Republic means by fiscal capacity.

Korda is present in the evidence and absent from the decision

Korda appears in the submissions because its government has documented infrastructure backlog and service obligations. Korda is also in the middle of a separate Territory Convention that could change its future status or geography.

The memorandum is careful not to merge those two processes.

Nothing in the equalization review assumes that Korda remains territorially unchanged. Nothing treats the Convention as proof that the equalization formula failed. And nothing promises that a new formula would erase the backlog that helped make equalization politically salient there in the first place.

That separation is constitutionally mundane and politically important. The Convention decides Korda's status question under §15.5.a. The Legislature decides the statutory equalization formula under §12.6. One process may change the facts the other eventually has to apply, but neither gets to decide the other's question.

Thoss can claim process, not a result

Civic Consul Elin Thoss sent the equalization question back into ordinary legislative review rather than attempting to move individual requests by executive discretion. That choice is increasingly visible as a governing position: if the rules are producing a bad distribution, change the rules in public.

It is not yet a policy victory.

The Legislature owns whatever statute comes next. The Executive Monitor remains independent and has made no failure finding. The committee can still recommend no amendment. And even if members settle on a new factor, they will still have to decide definitions, weights, data sources and transition rules before the change can operate.

For now, the useful development is narrower.

The committee no longer has to ask only whether Territories have described a real problem. It now has to decide which constitutional and fiscal problem, if any, belongs inside the formula.

That is where the argument was always going.
