State Constitutional History · Kelvant Test 03 · Year 10, Month 07

The Sixty-Day Answer

At the end of a wet summer, residents in a small Kelvant lake-shore county petitioned their county council to repair the access road to the public landing. The lane took school buses and ambulances through the same low crossing where fishing crews moved their gear. The petition asked for a funded reconstruction schedule, not merely another inspection.

The clerk accepted and registered it. A committee considered the road's condition, heard from the county engineer, and requested a cost estimate. By the fifty-eighth day, however, the only public response was a note that the subject would appear at a later budget session. Petition organizers argued that such a note was not an answer to the substance of their request.

Receipt is not an answer

Under Kelvant §1.13, residents were entitled to a public, substantive answer within sixty days, not to an automatic approval of what they requested. Section 5.4 required the governing body to maintain a process for recording and answering petitions. Two organizers brought a narrowly framed action in the State court, asking for compliance with those duties rather than an order appropriating money for the road.

On the sixtieth day, the council published a response. It identified the damage, set out the engineer's preliminary estimate, explained why the current capital budget could pay only for temporary drainage work, and committed to considering reconstruction against other projects in the next budget cycle. The petitioners still disagreed with the decision, but the response at least addressed what they had asked.

The court treated the statutory process and the constitutional right as distinct. Publication of a receipt alone would not have met the substantive-response requirement. A reasoned refusal or deferral, supported by the council's stated considerations, could. On the record before it the council had provided a substantive response within the sixty-day period, leaving no unfulfilled duty for the court to compel.

What the right did not decide

At the next budget hearing the petitioners returned with the estimate and challenged the priorities used to defer the work. They did so as participants in local government, not as holders of a judicially guaranteed repair appropriation. The Constitution had forced the council to answer them. It had not supplied the council's decision.
