State Constitutional History · Kelvant Test 02 · Year 11, Month 5

A Year for the Shore

Kelvant's Legislature enacted the State Service and Militia Act after years of arguing over the lake shore's disaster readiness and the practical value of public service. The measure required eligible adult residents to complete a paid term in the State Militia: twelve months full-time or an equivalent part-time schedule, including a route compatible with higher education. The State promised instruction, actual assigned work, and wages. Supporters called it a shared civic obligation; opponents said the State had no right to put unwilling residents into a military organization.

Rather than amend the State Constitution, both houses passed the Act through their ordinary legislative process. They relied on the existing command and militia provisions of Kelvant's Constitution and on the Republic's rules permitting State forces. The Act created a limited career establishment with authorized staffing and leadership billets set by law, while placing ordinary recruitment, promotion, and selection for continued professional employment in the chain of command. Completing required service would confer no permanent post or promotion.

Two routes to the same obligation

The Act directed the responsible department to establish equal aggregate service requirements for the full-time and part-time routes, with scheduling accommodations not contingent on attending college. It required compensation for duty, individual fitness assessment, access to medical accommodations, and an application process for reassignment to noncombatant specialties where conscience or disability made another assignment inappropriate. Those who could not perform any available duties could seek a reasoned exemption subject to independent review. Neither a declaration of religious belief nor a declaration of support for the Militia was a condition of assignment.

Much of the work was not armed duty. Members trained in rescue logistics, communications, engineering support, shelter coordination and protection of essential infrastructure. The statute barred the use of a compulsory-service placement as a source of labor for a private employer without consent. It did not authorize ordinary police powers simply by enrolling or activating a member.

The challenge

Three residents sought judicial review. One opposed all compelled service as an intrusion upon liberty. Another accepted community duties but objected in conscience to bearing arms. The third argued that the part-time route had been advertised chiefly to university students, leaving residents who worked or cared for family without an equally practical way to complete the obligation.

The State answered that the federal Constitution expressly excepted lawful military conscription from the forced-labor prohibition in §1.4, that §14.5 authorized organized State forces for disaster response, civil emergency and preservation of public order, and that Kelvant §§1.5 and 3.13 directed the Legislature to establish a militia framework by law. The challengers replied that none of those provisions eliminated the independent guarantees of conscience, equality, and due process.

The court agreed with both propositions in part. It declined to treat the federal conscription exception as a freestanding grant of unlimited State authority, but held that a generally applicable, compensated term in a lawfully constituted State Militia was not unconstitutional solely because it was compulsory. The Act's public purposes matched the permitted domestic purposes of State forces, and the Legislature—not a unilateral gubernatorial order—had defined the obligation, funded it, and prescribed limits.

The judgment also made the law's protections enforceable. Noncombatant assignment requests had to receive individual consideration, with written reasons and review; no person could be compelled to profess a belief or political loyalty. An equivalent part-time schedule could not be withheld merely because a person was outside higher education. Fitness rules and exemptions had to be applied under neutral standards, without arbitrary exclusion or punishment. The court left questions about the legality of any specific activation, assignment or sanction open to later review on their facts.

Command without patronage

Governor Elian Rusk subsequently presented honorary service medals to members who had distinguished themselves during a difficult shoreline evacuation exercise. He also sent a written recommendation that a communications specialist be considered for advancement. The professional selection board considered the recommendation alongside the specialist's record; it remained free to reject advancement under its lawful criteria and the statutory complement of leadership billets. A decoration conferred neither command authority nor a career appointment.

The Governor remained commander-in-chief while the force served Kelvant. A deployment still required the published legal basis, purpose, geographic scope and expected duration required by State §2.9. Activation by itself gave members no general law-enforcement authority. Federal call-up, if ever lawful, would follow the separate restrictions of the Republic's §14.5 rather than a State officer's unilateral preference.

What a year meant

The first intake was not a permanent army in miniature. Most members would complete their paid obligation and return to ordinary life, free to describe their training and experience as they saw fit. A smaller number could apply for full-time professional service in positions lawfully authorized and funded. The State owed neither an appointment nor an exemption from normal selection rules.

Kelvant's experiment survived its first constitutional challenge within defined limits. The State could ask for a year of service, but it could not demand a creed, invent powers through activation, make educational privilege a gate to flexibility, or treat the uniform as an escape from constitutional review.
