State Constitutional History · Corindal Test 02 · Year 7, Month 11

The Entry That Stayed Visible

In Year 7, Month 11, a State transport board discovered that the Corindal Register showed a unanimous vote approving a revised stop-access plan. The minutes submitted by the board's clerk had transposed two agenda numbers. The recorded vote belonged to an unrelated maintenance contract; the stop-access plan had been deferred for additional consultation.

The mistake mattered to a group of residents who had already begun preparing comments on the proposed changes. A contractor cited the published entry as evidence that the plan was final. The board's chair asked the registry office to replace the erroneous posting with the corrected minutes and remove the original to avoid public confusion.

A correction is not erasure

The registrar refused to overwrite the entry silently. Corindal State §8.1 required the Register to preserve official acts and State-board proceedings, and §8.1(d) prohibited silent alteration, destruction, or permanent concealment of a recorded act. That provision did not forbid correction; it required the original entry and the record of the change to survive.

The board submitted an attested correction explaining which agenda references had been transposed and confirming that no decision on the stop-access proposal had occurred at that meeting. The registrar published the correction beside the original document with its date and source, marked the earlier description as inaccurate, and linked both to the authenticated board minutes. Readers could see what the public had first been told and what the board had actually done.

Public reliance

Residents requested a fresh consultation timetable, pointing to the possibility that others had assumed the plan was already approved. The board opened a new notice period before taking any vote on the plan. It did not treat the contractor's reliance on the mistaken entry as a substitute for lawful board approval.

The correction did not turn an administrative error into an unlawful substantive decision; it prevented an error from becoming invisible. Corindal's public record remained a history of official conduct, including documented mistakes, rather than an always-polished account that could be rewritten after the fact.
