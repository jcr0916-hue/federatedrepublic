State Constitutional History · Orath Test 01 · Year 8, Month 4

The Petition at Harvest

In the fields outside Dorven, a cooperative could ask a person to work through every harvest without ever promising to admit that worker as a member. Orath's Constitution did not erase that distinction. It did, however, require fair consideration when a qualifying worker petitioned to join. In Year 8, that promise met a test at the Millford Grain Cooperative.

A refusal without reasons

Elra Vennic had worked for the cooperative through successive planting and harvest seasons. After completing the continuous service period required by the statute then in force, she submitted a petition for membership. The cooperative board refused her application in a short notice that offered no grounds. Other workers with comparable service had recently been admitted.

Vennic asked for the reasons and the membership criteria applied to her petition. The board answered that its charter left admission entirely to its discretion. She did not demand that a court award her a share of the cooperative. Her claim was narrower: under State §1.5, substantial lawful work entitled her to fair consideration on terms no less favorable than those offered to similarly situated applicants, and a denial had to state its grounds in writing.

The tribunal could require a process, not a result

The dispute went to an Orath Labor Arbitration Tribunal, a forum expressly empowered by §4.4 to hear cooperative-participation claims. Its hearing gave the board and Vennic an opportunity to present the cooperative charter, admission records, and the reasons the board said justified different treatment.

The tribunal found that the unexplained refusal did not satisfy the Constitution's requirement for written grounds. It also found that the board had not shown how the criteria it invoked had been applied to the comparable applicants. It set aside the defective decision and ordered the cooperative to reconsider the petition under its lawful rules, using disclosed criteria and a written decision.

The tribunal refused Vennic's request for immediate admission. Section 1.5 protected a fair decision-making process; it did not make membership automatic after a specified number of harvests. A cooperative remained free to deny a petition for lawful, fairly applied reasons.

Work continued while the petition returned

After the hearing, a supervisor proposed removing Vennic from the next scheduled loading rotation because she had taken the case outside the cooperative. Vennic invoked §1.12, which prohibits retaliation for lawfully asserting cooperative-participation rights. The proposed removal was withdrawn, and she remained eligible for work under the same scheduling rules as others.

On reconsideration, the board issued a new written decision and admitted Vennic to membership. That outcome rested on the board's reassessment, not on a judicial power to assign cooperative shares. The public lesson from Millford was therefore twofold: a cooperative could preserve genuine membership standards, but it could not conceal unequal treatment behind a bare assertion of discretion or punish a worker for asking that those standards be applied.
