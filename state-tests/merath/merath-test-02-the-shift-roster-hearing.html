State Constitutional History · Merath Test 02 · Year 10, Month 9

The Shift Roster Hearing

At a metal-finishing plant in Merath's industrial belt, employees discovered that the shift roster posted at the gate differed from the payroll record supplied to the State inspector. Several workers said the missing hours coincided with a maintenance shutdown, when the same crews had been asked to clear solvent residues before restarting the line.

The workers filed a joint claim before a Labor Tribunal under State §4.4. They sought an accounting of hours, payment for work actually performed, and an order requiring a safety review of the shutdown procedure. The employer disputed the hours and said the cleanup had been carried out by a separate contractor. The workers did not have to obtain the employer's permission to invoke the tribunal's jurisdiction.

A hearing accessible to both sides

The tribunal waived advance filing costs that would have materially deterred the workers' claims. It arranged a hearing schedule that allowed the affected shift teams to attend and required the parties to exchange the records they intended to rely upon. Accessibility did not make the employer's evidence irrelevant: the tribunal admitted the contractor's work orders and permitted questioning of the workers about the timing of their shifts.

After examining the timestamped gate register, maintenance instructions and pay records, the tribunal found that several workers had been assigned work during periods shown as unpaid in the payroll system. It ordered the employer to correct the wage records and make the payments required under existing law. The tribunal also directed the appropriate safety authority to examine the shutdown procedure, while declining to find a separate safety violation on the incomplete evidence before it.

The route beyond the tribunal

The employer appealed the wage order to Merath's High Court under §4.4(e), arguing that the tribunal had treated the gate register as conclusive and disregarded the contractor's evidence. The High Court held that the tribunal had considered both sets of records and supplied reasons for crediting the workers' evidence for the identified shifts. It affirmed the wage order without converting the unresolved safety allegation into a finding.

The workers obtained relief through a specialized court, but not through a tribunal empowered to accept every claim without proof. The employer received notice, an opportunity to contest evidence, and appellate review. Merath's constitutional commitment to accessible labor justice operated alongside the ordinary requirements of impartiality and due process.
