State Constitutional History · Harren Test 01 · Year 11, Month 3

The Order Neither Executive Could Give

The State Infrastructure Board proposed a Northern Highway Upgrade combining two kinds of work. Widening and resurfacing the existing highway had already been authorized and appropriated under the Highway Maintenance Act. Converting three sections to toll-free corridors and building a statewide electric-vehicle charging network had not. The Board packaged them together because the construction work overlapped.

Two executives, two claims

Governor Aric Vane presented the full project as a political priority and directed the Board to begin all components. State Administrator Selia Kars refused to execute the order. Under §§2.1 and 2.7, she argued, carrying out existing law belonged to the administrative sphere, while the new toll and charging policies required legislative authority. The Governor could recommend them under §2.3, but could not create them by directing an administrative board.

The officers consulted and failed to agree. Kars petitioned the High Court under §2.8(c). While the dispute was pending, §2.8(d) prevented either officer from taking the contested action except as necessary for safety, continuity, existing legal obligations, or protection of State property. Routine road-surface repairs, traffic signals, and emergency-call equipment continued; new construction tied to the disputed policy did not.

The Court draws the boundary

The High Court ruled within the constitutional fourteen-day period. Justice Mira Vale, writing for the Court, treated the project as divisible rather than assigning the entire subject to one executive. The already authorized widening and resurfacing were implementation of existing law and therefore belonged to the State Administrator. The toll-free designation and statewide charging network were new policy choices. They required legislative authorization and, where public money was required, appropriation through the fiscal process described in §7.3.

The Court also rejected the Governor’s attempt to use political leadership as a power of direct administrative command. Harren’s Constitution allowed the Governor to publish priorities and recommend measures; it did not make the Administrator subordinate. At the same time, the decision gave Kars no authority to create the new transportation policy herself.

Compliance without subordination

Vane withdrew the directive and announced that he would place the toll-free and charging proposals in his next fiscal-priorities statement and advocate for legislation. Kars resumed the already authorized highway work under the existing appropriation. Neither office acquired the other’s powers.

The decision was published in the Harren State Record under §9.2. The episode became the State’s clearest early example of §2.8 in operation: when political direction and administrative execution overlap, the High Court may draw the line without turning either executive into the superior of the other.
