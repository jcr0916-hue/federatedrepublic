State Constitutional History · Arvane Test 01 · Year 11, Month 8

The Rule That Ran Out

On Arvane’s western waterfront, a regulation governing the inspection of privately operated cargo-lifting equipment had become so familiar that operators treated it as permanent. It had been adopted almost ten years earlier under a lawful safety statute. Now the rule was approaching the outer limit of its constitutional life, and the responsible State office wanted to keep it.

Port businesses did not dispute the need to prevent crane failures. They disputed whether an old inspection requirement should continue in its existing form after newer testing and independent certification methods had become available. Arvane’s Constitution made that question one the government had to answer in public.

Renewal is not a stamp

The agency issued a short notice proposing to continue the regulation, saying only that the existing rule had maintained safe operations. The notice named the rule but supplied no current comparison of inspection methods, no assessment of costs and benefits, and no explanation of why less burdensome means would not be sufficient.

A group of smaller freight operators requested the evidence required by §8.2(c). They proposed that recognized private certification, backed by enforceable safety standards and targeted inspection, might meet the same legitimate purpose. The Constitution did not require the State to accept that alternative automatically. It did require an explanation of why reasonably available alternatives were inadequate before renewing a mandatory rule.

The office held a public comment period, but it treated the request for fresh justification as an objection to safety itself. It published a one-sentence renewal determination shortly before the rule’s ten-year limit. The determination cited the rule’s past existence, not a current review of its necessity.

A challenge before the deadline

Harbor equipment operator Ilen Marr brought a challenge under §8.2(d), asking the court to determine whether the purported renewal complied with Article VIII. Marr did not ask the court to abolish safety regulation, only to apply the standards the Constitution set for continuing it.

The court held that an assertion of continuing usefulness was not the public, evidence-based renewal contemplated by §8.4. The office had not supplied the current statutory authority, expected costs and benefits, supporting evidence, or explanation of less burdensome means required by §8.2(c). The purported renewal therefore did not extend the regulation’s life.

When the ten-year sunset arrived, the old regulation expired. Inspectors could still enforce valid statutory safety requirements and other rules that remained in force, but they could not impose penalties based solely on the expired equipment rule. Nor could its continued appearance in old guidance give it renewed legal effect.

The State could regulate again, lawfully

The agency returned to the ordinary process with a new proposal. It opened at least thirty days of public comment and published an updated safety analysis, the basis of its delegated authority, and its reasons for accepting some certification alternatives while retaining certain direct inspections. The final rule was published to the Arvane State Record before ordinary enforcement began.

The new requirements were not identical to those that had expired. Some operators faced fewer duplicate checks; equipment presenting documented risks remained subject to direct inspection. The agency’s revised choices could still be challenged, but they rested on a stated current justification rather than the mere survival of an old rule.

Arvane’s ten-year limit did not mean that safety rules must disappear forever. It meant that keeping a rule required the State to explain why it still needed that rule, on the record, before the clock ran out.
