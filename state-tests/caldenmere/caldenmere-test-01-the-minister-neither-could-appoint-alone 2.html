State Constitutional History · Caldenmere Test 01 · Year 9, Month 10

The Minister Neither Could Appoint Alone

At Calden harbor, a vacancy in the Ministry of Maritime Affairs became a test of Caldenmere’s divided executive. The President of the State Senate, Corlan Vey, wanted an experienced port administrator to lead the Ministry. Governor Selda Marr objected to the recommendation and announced that she preferred a different candidate. Both officers spoke as if their own part of the appointment process should settle the whole matter.

Neither held that power. Caldenmere gives the Governor ceremonial representation and formal appointment duties, but entrusts administrative executive authority to the President of the State Senate. A Minister must be appointed by the Governor upon recommendation of the Senate President.

A nomination was not an appointment

Vey sent the Governor a formal recommendation naming port administrator Mara Tellen. The Governor did not issue the appointment. Instead, she published a statement identifying her preferred candidate and asked the Ministry’s senior staff to recognize that person as its prospective head.

The staff refused to treat a public preference as a completed appointment. Under §§2.3(d) and 2.8, the Governor could not appoint a Minister of her own choosing without the Senate President’s recommendation. Equally, Vey’s recommendation alone did not place Tellen in office: Caldenmere’s constitutional text required a gubernatorial act of appointment.

Routine administration continued under existing lawful departmental arrangements. But the unresolved vacancy prevented either officer from presenting a proposed Minister as already installed.

The decree could not cure the vacancy

Vey then issued an Administrative Decree directing the Ministry to recognize Tellen as its Minister pending the Governor’s action. The decree invoked the Senate President’s authority to supervise Ministers and implement State policy. Opponents challenged it before the courts.

The court distinguished the implementation of existing law from the appointment of a constitutional officer. Section 2.5(c) permitted Administrative Decrees only to implement statute; a decree could not replace the specific procedure established by §2.8. It could not make a recommendation into an appointment or give the recommended candidate powers that had not lawfully vested.

The decree’s purported installation of Tellen was held ineffective. The decision did not choose the better Minister or transfer appointment power to the judiciary. It left the two constitutional officers with their respective responsibilities.

Two signatures, two functions

With the distinction made public, Vey maintained his recommendation and the Governor eventually issued the formal appointment of Tellen. The appointment was entered in the Caldenmere Registry. Once in office, Tellen led Maritime Affairs under the administrative supervision of the Senate President, not under independent direction from the Governor.

The resolution was not a victory for a single executive. The Governor performed the required formal act; the Senate President supplied the recommendation and retained operational supervision. Caldenmere’s separation proved capable of producing delay and public disagreement, but neither a speech nor an Administrative Decree could erase one half of the constitutional process.
