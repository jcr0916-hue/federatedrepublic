The Convention · Korda

The Night the Conditions Became Commitments

The missing votes were not won by pretending there was less to lose. They were won by changing what the settlement would require before anything could change hands.

Mara IsetThe Korda Press·KordaYear 13, Month 12

Dessa Orin did not agree to give up Korda's Statehood petition. She agreed that the Convention could address it without pretending Korda's geography was frozen forever.

That distinction emerged late in a long amendment session on the Northern Lake Settlement. Mire and Rell's draft already promised that only the northernmost three certified corridor districts would be placed on a path toward Kelvant, with the southern lakefront retained. Orin had challenged what that promise was worth if the institutions that connected the southern shore to inland Korda were divided or left without funding.

The answer was an amendment in the other direction from a typical political escape clause. Where the proposed text had required a future implementation agreement to identify an operator for each affected service, the revised clause made continuous lawful responsibility a condition of any actual transfer. A public transition schedule, completed and accepted by the authorities lawfully responsible, would have to show who would operate each affected service before jurisdictions changed. Existing obligations would remain where the law placed them until a lawful successor assumed them.

Unresolved titles and disputed shared costs would stay explicitly disputed. They could not be invented away, transferred by assumption, or used to leave a public facility unoperated. If the necessary commitments could not be reached through the constitutional approval process, the proposed boundary would not simply take effect by default.

What each side accepted

Rell said the change made it possible to distinguish a smaller northern departure from an abandonment of the remainder. He still did not know the final allocation of every cost. But he could tell his district that no change of sovereignty would be allowed to erase accountability for a service while accountants argued over who owned the building.

Mire accepted a clause requiring the authentic fixed district schedule to be included with the affected-voter ballot materials. His original petition's supporters might reject the narrower three-district result. They would not, he said, be asked to ratify a phrase that could later be stretched north or south by administrative convenience. No voter who signed for the full corridor had yet voted on the actual settlement the Convention was now constructing.

Orin accepted no guarantee of immediate Statehood, because the Constitution supplies none. Her amended language instead preserved the pending Statehood claim for the remaining Korda Territory under the audit process and its ordinary time limits. The Monitors would still have to evaluate the Territory that actually exists after any eventual boundary change. No earlier failed, incomplete or geographically mismatched audit could be counted as successful by declaring it so.

Orin told reporters she would support the revised proposal when it reached the Convention floor. It was not, she said, the future she had asked her neighbors to sign for. It was a route by which Korda's remaining communities could continue to seek that future without handing away their southern lake shore.

The persuader who made no announcement

Throughout the sitting, Assembly Speaker Elin Thoss occupied no speaking role in the Convention. She was not one of Korda's thirty-three delegates. She had been talking to members privately for several days, and on this evening several delegates who had objected to the first text were willing to work on precise conditions rather than denounce the entire proposal.

One delegate described her contribution only as a question: what would your constituents need written into the record before they could be asked to vote? Another said she had asked whether the other side could live with that same sentence. Neither claimed Thoss had written the final bargain or promised anything on behalf of the Senate, Kelvant or the Monitors.

The amendment remains the Convention's work. It was read and debated by members elected from Korda districts and can be approved or rejected only by them.

Still a choice, not a result

Seven delegates indicated they might oppose the resolution for different reasons. Some believed the certified corridor petition should not be narrowed. Others saw any territorial departure as too high a price for an uncertain Statehood path. Supporters refused to release a final count before the recorded vote.

Orin's public support changes the political prospect. It does not change the Constitution. The Convention must adopt the amended text if it wants a resolution; affected citizens must later ratify it; Kelvant and the other approving institutions must still act under §15.4; and the remaining Korda Territory must still prove Statehood capacity under §15.2.

The chamber has stopped asking whether a compromise can be described. Tomorrow, or at the next sitting the delegates set, it will have to decide whether that compromise is good enough to put to its people.
