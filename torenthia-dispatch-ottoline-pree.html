Dispatches from the Count Room

Ottoline Pree is deputy registrar of Weller County, in Selvane. She has run the county count room through six elections and has watched three tabulation systems arrive and leave. She did not set out to write about elections. A candidate asked her one question, and she answered at more length than it was asked.

Year 14 · Month 1

Rank Them All

Every two years the State sends me to a regional hall to be taught again how to count, and every two years I am the woman in the third row who has done it before and is expected to look interested. This year the instructor was a young man with a laminated flowchart, which I respected. A flowchart is a promise that someone has thought about the awkward case in advance.

For those who have only ever seen the answer on the evening report, here is what a ranked count is. Each voter ranks the candidates in order. We count the first choices. If one name has more than half, that is the end of it. If none does, the name with the fewest first choices is eliminated, and every ballot that had that name on top is counted again for whichever remaining name the voter ranked next. Then we look again. We do this until someone holds a majority of what is left. It is slow, it is on paper, and anyone who wants to may watch every step. The watching is not a courtesy. It is the method.

Last week a woman came to the Weller County office and asked to see the count room. She turned out to be Tamsin Hadley, who is on the ballot for the Senate seat Selvane fills this spring. The seat has had the same senator for twelve years, which is as long as the Constitution allows, so for the first time in a dozen years nobody on the ballot gets to say "incumbent." Hadley was a county auditor in the next county over, so she knew what a count room was. She wanted to see ours.

She asked three questions. How long does it take? As long as it takes. Could she send someone to watch? Anyone may, and so may her opponents, and they will watch each other, which is the most reliable audit I know. Would she know the result on the night? I told her what I tell everyone: you will know what the first choices say on the night, and you will know who won when the count is finished, and sometimes those are the same hour and sometimes they are not. She wrote that down. I liked her for it.

I do not tell people how I vote, and I would ask you not to guess. What I will say is that a Senate seat runs six years, and in six years I will count this one exactly once. I would like to count it right. Most of what keeps a county count honest is dull: two people at every table, a form for every box, a rule that nobody carries a ballot alone. Dull is the product.

She left her card. I put it in the drawer with the cards of everyone else who has ever asked to see the count room. It is a short stack. I hope it is taller by spring.
