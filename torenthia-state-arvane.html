The western shore's choice

“We did not come to this coast to be managed, directed, or improved by our government. We came to be free.”

Arvane occupies the Republic’s northwestern and western coast. Its established commercial center is Westport, a port whose freight connections and maritime businesses help define the State’s public identity. The Constitution’s own subtitle is blunt: Free Port — Open for Business. Where some States build their institutions around social guarantees or a balance of regional interests, Arvane places limited government, private exchange, and dependable contract enforcement at the center of its constitutional design.

Its preamble also draws a firm boundary: Arvane remains a State of the Federated Republic and acknowledges federal constitutional requirements. Its commitment to fewer State restrictions is a choice made within the Republic’s constitutional framework, not a claim to be exempt from it. State Article I expressly retains the federal rights floor, including applicable social protections. (Arvane Constitution, Preamble and §§1.1, 1.14.)

A Legislature that meets only when necessary

Arvane has a unicameral Assembly. Its members serve two-year terms, with a limit of six consecutive terms before a full-term break, and vacancies must be filled by special election rather than appointment. The Assembly meets in ordinary session only once every two years: the first Monday in February of each odd-numbered year. A regular session lasts at most ninety calendar days, with a single extension of up to thirty days by a majority of the full authorized membership. Unfinished business lapses when the session ends. (State §§2.1–2.4.)

That deliberately narrow legislative calendar is coupled to rules for the legislation that does get passed. A bill must concern one subject, its complete text must be public for at least three days before final passage, and a substantial amendment restarts that waiting period. The Governor can call special sessions only within the constitutional process. New or increased taxes require two-thirds of the Assembly’s full authorized membership. (State §§2.5, 2.7, 2.9.)

A Governor, but not one commanding every officer

The Governor is elected statewide for four years and may serve no more than two consecutive terms without sitting out a term. The office directs administration within its own constitutional domain, represents Arvane in intergovernmental relations, reports to the Assembly and coordinates lawful emergency action. The Constitution does not make every other elected executive officer subordinate to the Governor. (State §§3.1–3.3.)

The Attorney General, Secretary of Elections, State Treasurer and State Controller are independently elected on staggered four-year cycles. The Attorney General retains independent legal judgment. The Treasurer holds State funds and reports on the Treasury. The Controller independently audits accounts and certifies fiscal limits and compliance. This arrangement divides executive and financial authority without multiplying appointed constitutional commissions. (State §3.7.)

Commercial justice and the free port

Arvane makes commercial predictability a constitutional function. Its specialized Commercial Court hears contract and business disputes; proceedings are to be resolved as promptly as practicable and ordinarily within 180 days. Missing that target does not end the court’s jurisdiction or invalidate a case. The Constitution also protects lawful private arbitration agreements, subject to fraud, coercion, illegality, procedural unfairness, and other general legal defenses. (State §§4.5, 4.8.)

The Legislature may designate Free Port Zones for expedited trade and logistics, streamlined permitting and limited State business-tax advantages. Their benefits must be established by general law, are limited to ten years, and cannot be exclusive privileges for favored enterprises. Federal law continues to govern federal customs and other federal matters. The zones express Arvane’s commercial philosophy without creating a jurisdiction outside the Republic. (State §5.4.)

Taxes that cannot be levied

The State may not levy personal income, estate, inheritance, gift, or intangible-personal-property taxes. Its annual spending limit generally follows the prior year's appropriation level adjusted for population growth and inflation, with the State Controller independently certifying the calculation. To exceed the limit, two-thirds of the full Assembly must make and publish an express fiscal-emergency finding. Excess revenues must be returned or reserved as law provides. (State §§7.2, 7.4.)

The budget must rest on reasonably anticipated revenues. A shortfall requires reporting by the Treasurer and independent certification by the Controller before expenditure reductions or new legislative steps proceed under lawful authority. Long-term debt is limited to capital improvements, needs a two-thirds Assembly vote and electoral ratification, and cannot exceed five percent of the assessed value of taxable property. The State generally may not operate a business in competition with private enterprise, except essential public infrastructure established by law. (State §§7.5, 7.8–7.9.)

Regulation must justify itself

Arvane's constitutional test for regulation favors the least burdensome reasonably available means of preventing fraud and protecting health, safety, rights or comparable substantial public interests. Where voluntary compliance or private certification can serve that purpose, the Constitution directs officials to prefer it. Regulations normally expire within ten years unless publicly reviewed and reauthorized on current grounds. Emergency rules have a narrower temporary route, subject to publication and prompt ordinary review. (State §§8.1–8.4.)

The Arvane State Record is the durable, freely accessible record of State law, executive action and constitutional certifications. Ordinarily a law or regulation cannot be enforced against someone before official publication. This is a State institution, operating alongside rather than in place of the Republic’s National Record System. (State §§9.1–9.4.)

Read Arvane's institutions for yourself

Arvane's design is more than a slogan about commerce. A short legislative session, separately elected financial officers, enforceable publication rules and a public vote on long-term debt place practical limits around its preference for small government. They also create real questions for voters: how much regulation and expenditure are enough to maintain the rights and services federal and State law require, and who is accountable when a narrow government has to act?
