The Stalemate

Year 7. The Legat Consul concludes a mutual defense treaty requiring domestic infrastructure commitments. The Civic Consul refuses to authorize the spending on fiscal grounds. Both are acting within their constitutional domain. Neither will yield.

§2.14.a Cross-Domain Emergency Coordination §2.14 Council of Ministers §2.4 Consular Domain §3.7 Legislative Process §10.1 National Record System

The Meridian Mutual Defense Treaty committed the Republic to a fifteen-year program of joint infrastructure investment: hardened communication nodes, shared satellite ground stations, and a network of dual-use logistics facilities along the northern territorial boundary. The Legat Consul concluded the treaty in Year 6, with Senate ratification following in Year 7. The treaty's domestic implementation provisions — the infrastructure spending itself — required parliamentary appropriation.

The Civic Consul read the MA's preliminary cost assessment and refused to authorize the spending request. Her fiscal objection was specific: the treaty's Year 8–12 commitments would coincide with the Republic's structural deficit correction window, the period during which three consecutive Satisfactory MA certifications were required before any new major capital commitment could be added to the medium-term fiscal framework. The Civic Consul was not disputing the Legat Consul's authority to conclude the treaty. She was declining to authorize spending that her fiscal framework could not accommodate.

The Legat Consul's position was equally precise. The treaty was ratified. It was the law of the Republic. The domestic infrastructure commitments were treaty obligations, not discretionary spending. The Civic Consul's fiscal objection — however legitimate as fiscal policy — did not override a ratified treaty commitment. The Legat Consul was not asking the Civic Consul to violate her fiscal framework. He was stating that the Republic had a binding international obligation that the domestic fiscal framework would have to accommodate.

Both were right. Neither was wrong. The constitutional machinery had no obvious answer.

Why the Standard Mechanisms Failed

The disagreement concerned how to fund and implement a treaty, not who held the treaty-negotiation power. §2.14 provided coordination through the Council of Ministers. §2.14.a addressed temporary operational leadership in a cross-domain emergency; it did not create a thirty-day legislative arbitration process for this fiscal dispute. A genuine constitutional-domain dispute could go to the SC under §4.5.

The Council of Ministers had been working on the problem for four months before the public dispute notice. The joint working group had identified three possible bridging mechanisms: a phased implementation schedule that deferred the most expensive commitments beyond the deficit correction window, a contingency appropriation that would activate automatically on MA certification of structural balance, and a treaty renegotiation to extend the implementation timeline. The Legat Consul found the first two inadequate to meet the treaty's Year 8 benchmarks. The Civic Consul found the third diplomatically unacceptable — renegotiating a ratified mutual defense treaty over domestic fiscal concerns would signal a constitutional unreliability she was not willing to create.

§2.14 — Public Record of an Unresolved Dispute
Day 127

On Day 127 the Legat Consul published the unresolved treaty-implementation dispute and the Council’s work to the NRS. Publication informed the Legislature; it did not activate a constitutional deadline, transfer either executive’s domain, or permit a concurrent resolution to appropriate funds outside the ordinary budget process.

Members of both chambers began drafting an implementation bill. They set a thirty-day political target, not a constitutional deadline. Appropriations and revenue changes still required ordinary legislation.

The Civic Consul published her fiscal concerns and offered to support legislation that addressed the treaty commitments and their budget consequences. The Republic’s international obligations and domestic appropriation requirements both remained relevant.

The Concurrent Resolution

The Legislature could resolve the funding policy by law within its competence. It could not permanently alter constitutional executive domains through an ordinary resolution. Missing the members’ target would not automatically transfer treaty authority to the Civic Consul.

The implementation bill developed over 26 days authorized a contingency appropriation through the ordinary budget process, with the specified fiscal conditions. It called for a revised implementation schedule consistent with the Republic’s treaty obligations and a domestic offset proposal. Any change to international commitments still had to be agreed through the applicable treaty process.

Ordinary Legislation — Implementation Agreement
Day 167

The implementation bill passed both chambers and became law on Day 167. Its statutory terms governed the appropriation and reporting duties. The executives retained their constitutional domains, working through the Council on implementation.

The Legat Consul submitted the revised implementation schedule on Day 221. The Civic Consul presented the offset mechanism on Day 248. The Assembly approved it 289-211. The original fiscal authorization dispute was resolved through ordinary legislative process — which is exactly where the constitution intended it to land.

What This Scenario Tests

§2.14.a supplies a narrow emergency coordination mechanism: either executive may propose a temporary operational lead, both may concur, and disagreement may be referred to the SC. It does not alter either domain. The non-emergency fiscal disagreement here was resolved through ordinary legislation and executive coordination.

There is no separate constitutional Coordination Failure Protocol with a thirty-day legislative clock or a default transfer to the Civic Consul. The public record made this disagreement visible while the ordinary institutions worked toward a lawful solution.

Publishing the unresolved dispute carried political consequences for both executives. Readers could compare the treaty obligations, fiscal concerns, Council proposals, and eventual legislation. The NRS supplied accountability without creating new decision-making authority.

The Meridian Treaty was ultimately implemented — late, restructured, and through a more laborious process than the Legat Consul had intended. The constitutional order held. The impasse was resolved not by one executive yielding to the other but by the Legislature performing the function that representative institutions exist to perform: resolving genuine political disagreements between constitutionally valid positions.
