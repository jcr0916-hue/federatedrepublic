The Critical Finding

Year 11. The LM identifies systematic overstatement of revenue projections. The Legislature must publish a response within the statutory period, but may disagree with the finding and choose its own policy. A separate suspension procedure protects oversight from retaliation.

§9.5.a Compliance Standards and Findings §9.1.a The Legislative Monitor §9.7.a Institutional Compromise Protocol §10.1 National Record System

The LM's Year 11 annual legislative audit measured the Legislature's revenue-projection accuracy against the fiscal-accuracy standard the JMC had published for that audit category the year before — a standard the JMC, not the LM alone, had defined and made public in advance, together with the methodology behind it. Revenue projections had overstated collections in seven of the prior nine budget cycles, by an average of 6.2%. Against the published standard, the aspect failed.

The Fail was published to the NRS on a Wednesday, under §9.5.a, and reported to Assembly leadership — the body responsible for that aspect. §9.5.a required the responsible body to publish its own account within the period defined by statute. It did not require agreement with the finding or a prescribed remediation plan. The finding existed permanently and publicly, on a record built from a standard the Assembly had known about since before the audit began.

§9.5.a — No Mandatory Remedy
The Legislature must respond. It may disagree, and the original record remains.

Assembly leaders disputed the forecast methodology. The earlier published standard made the disagreement examinable, but did not make the JMC’s view binding or prevent later criticism. The Assembly had to answer the finding within the statutory period; its answer could disagree. That political and evidentiary burden was different from a power in the Monitor to compel agreement.

Two months later the Assembly published its response within the applicable statutory period. It chose an independent forecasting review and MA fiscal analysis for the next three budget cycles, and invited LM comments before tabling. Neither a Monitor report nor its absence could be made a prerequisite to legislative business. These were policy and oversight choices, not a remedy imposed by the finding.

When the Auditor Fails — §9.7.a

The LM may publish fiscal-accuracy findings within its mandate. The Legislature can dispute their reasoning and must publish its response within the statutory period. Neither disagreement nor later response alters the original permanent record. The Monitor cannot make its preferred forecast methodology a constitutional precondition to legislation.

Under §9.7.a the other two Monitor Generals may publish findings recommending suspension for serious misconduct or abuse of office. For the LM, approval requires a majority of the Supreme Court. Their recommendation alone does not suspend authority. A valid suspension is limited to ninety days unless removal proceedings have begun, and any continued suspension remains subject to expedited independent review of necessity. Disagreement with the audit finding supplies no ground for it. Acting authority under §9.1.d follows only a valid interruption of authority.

What This Scenario Tests

The continuing audit made the revenue pattern visible without giving the LM governing authority. Doing nothing would still breach the statutory-period response duty and could be challenged through the available legal remedies. The Legislature’s choice of a methodology review was its own policy response; the EM and courts retained their separate functions.
