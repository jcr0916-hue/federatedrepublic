The Rate

Year 12. The Monetary Authority raises rates into a weak economy. The elected branches hate the decision and discover that controlling an institution's structure is not the same thing as controlling its judgment.

Inflation had fallen for six consecutive months, unemployment had risen, and a national election was nine months away. The Monetary Authority nevertheless raised its policy rate by 1.25 percentage points after concluding that wage and credit data showed inflation would otherwise return the following year.

The decision was lawful. It was also politically disastrous. The Civic Consul called it "an unelected recession." Assembly leaders announced that an institution created by the Constitution was not exempt from democratic control.

The Reform Bill

The Assembly introduced the Monetary Accountability and Reorganization Act. The bill did four things: reduced the Authority's governing board from seven principal decision-makers to three; terminated the terms of four sitting members on enactment; authorized the Civic Consul to appoint all three replacements subject to ordinary Senate confirmation; and permitted removal whenever a decision-maker's policies were found to be "materially contrary to national economic objectives."

§12.1 — Statute may organize; statute may not capture
The Constitution leaves machinery to law, but independence is a limit on what that law may do.

Changing committee names, staff organization, meeting procedures, reporting schedules, research offices, or the number of future seats could be legitimate statutory choices if they preserved the constitutional safeguards. The Constitution does not freeze an organizational chart.

But immediate termination of sitting protected terms was not merely organization. Giving one actor the ability to appoint a majority was not merely organization. Making lawful policy disagreement a removal ground was directly contrary to the constitutional independence rule.

The Compromise

The Senate refused the original bill. A revised act passed instead. It kept the seven-member board, consolidated two research bureaus, required quarterly public testimony, standardized publication of vote records, and changed the future appointment calendar so that vacancies remained staggered. Principal decision-makers still came through the independent candidate process and still required two-thirds Senate confirmation.

The revised act also created an inspector for procurement and administrative compliance. The inspector could audit contracts, disclosure systems, and internal controls. It could not revise a rate decision, direct currency issuance, or remove a principal decision-maker for economic judgment.

What elected government still controls
Independence is not institutional immunity.

The Legislature may define the Authority's internal organization and procedures, fund lawful operations, require transparency consistent with Article X, establish neutral administrative standards, and alter future structures prospectively. The Authority remains subject to courts, ethics rules, disclosure duties, and constitutional review.

What the elected branches cannot do is use those ordinary powers as a disguised substitute for controlling monetary judgment.

The Lawsuit

A group of legislators challenged the revised act from the opposite direction, arguing that any statutory reorganization of the Monetary Authority violated its independence. The Supreme Court rejected that claim. Independence protected the Authority's constitutional functions; it did not constitutionalize every office, bureau, committee, or reporting format the Legislature had previously created.

What This Scenario Tests

§12.1 draws a line between constitutional function and statutory form. The Monetary Authority is independent in the exercise of its constitutional responsibilities, but its internal machinery remains adaptable by statute.

The hard cases therefore ask what a change does, not what it is called. A reorganization that improves administration can be lawful. A reorganization designed to terminate protected terms, concentrate appointments in one actor, or punish lawful monetary judgment crosses the constitutional line.
