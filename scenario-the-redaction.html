Classification · Petition for Redacted Release · §10.2

The Redaction

Year 25. A procurement file hits its twenty-five year ceiling and the responsible executive extends it five more. A researcher does not challenge the extension. She asks for the parts that were never the secret — and the reasons for every line withheld.

§10.2 Classification Criteria §9.1 Monitor Information-Only Mandate §1.1 Right to Remedy §10.1 National Record System

The file is a defense procurement review from Year 1, classified on national security grounds under the statute that implements §10.2. It runs to four hundred pages: technical specifications, supplier assessments, a coastal facility's structural survey, cost projections, and the internal correspondence around a contract that was ultimately cancelled.

§10.2 sets a hard outer boundary on all of it. No classification may exceed twenty-five years, extendable once by five, and never beyond thirty under any circumstances. In Year 25 the ceiling arrives, and the responsible executive publishes a justification to the Executive Monitor and takes the single 5-year extension the provision allows. The file is now closed until Year 30 — which is also the absolute outer limit, so this is the last extension anyone can ever take on it.

The Executive Monitor assesses the justification, as §10.2 requires, and publishes a finding: the stated ground supports continued protection of the facility survey, and does not clearly support continued protection of the remainder.

What the Finding Does, and What It Does Not

Nothing, immediately. §9.1 is unambiguous — Monitor findings are not binding on any constitutional actor and are not a prerequisite for any legal proceeding or governmental function. The Executive Monitor observes and reports. It cannot order a page released.

The extension stands. The file stays closed. A researcher reading the National Record System that week can see the executive's justification, the Monitor's assessment that it is inadequate as to most of the file, and no mechanism connecting the two.

That was the gap. §10.2 now closes it from a different direction: not by giving the Monitor power, but by giving the finding somewhere to go.

The Petition She Actually Files

She does not challenge the extension. That route exists — any person with standing may petition for full declassification during an extension period, and the Monitor's finding would be admissible in that proceeding. But full declassification would require the court to conclude the stated ground no longer justifies classification of that record, and one part of the record is a structural survey of a facility that is still standing.

So she asks for the other three hundred and forty pages.

§10.2 — Petition for Redacted Release

Any person with standing may petition the applicable court for release of a classified record in redacted form, at any time during the classification period.

The court orders release of any portion whose disclosure would not itself satisfy the statutory ground under which the record was classified.

The standard is narrow on purpose. The government must justify each withheld portion on the same ground that justified classifying the record. It cannot classify four hundred pages over one sensitive survey and keep the rest dark by association. Before this route existed, §10.2 offered only a binary — fully closed until a ceiling expired, then fully open — which meant a single genuinely sensitive passage could hold an entire file for three decades.

What the Court Orders

The facility survey stays sealed; its disclosure plainly satisfies the ground the file was classified under. Sixty pages of supplier assessments stay sealed for the same reason. The cost projections, the cancelled-contract correspondence, and the technical specifications for equipment that has been publicly retired for a decade do not.

Three hundred and twelve pages are released. And then the provision does the thing that distinguishes it.

§10.2 — The Redactions Are Published Too

Where a record is released in redacted form, the redactions and the statutory ground for each are published to the NRS with the released portion.

The eighty-eight withheld pages appear on the National Record System as entries: page ranges, and for each, the statutory ground claimed. Not the contents. The shape of what is missing, and the reason given for each gap.

Why That Sentence Matters More Than the Release

Over-redaction is the ordinary failure of every disclosure regime. A record comes back with most of it blacked out, and the person who asked has no way to know whether the blackouts were necessary or convenient. The withholding is invisible in the only way that counts: you cannot argue with a decision you cannot see.

Publishing the redactions inverts that. Anyone can now see that eighty-eight pages were held, on which grounds, in which ranges. If forty of those pages were withheld on a ground that plainly does not fit — a cost table sealed as a structural survey — that mismatch is on the public record, and it is a fresh basis for a fresh petition. The government's redaction decisions become reviewable in the same way its classification decisions are.

This mirrors §10.1(6), which handles a related problem the same way: when the NRS Panel temporarily restricts a record for individual safety, the original timestamp and the restriction record both remain permanent public entries. The restriction is visible even when the content is not.

What Happens to the Rest

The facility survey and the supplier assessments remain classified until Year 30, when §10.2's absolute ceiling arrives. On that date the NRS publishes them automatically — this obligation is automatic and requires no further government act. No official has to decide. No petition is required. The record simply stops being closed.

The researcher does not petition again. The Executive Monitor's finding remains on the National Record System where it was published, having accomplished exactly what §9.1 permits it to accomplish: it told the public something true, and someone with standing carried it into a room where it counted for something.

What This Demonstrates

An oversight body that can compel is not an oversight body — it is a second executive. The Monitors are built to observe and report and nothing more, and that restraint is load-bearing. But a finding that goes nowhere is not restraint; it is decoration.

§10.2 resolves this without touching §9.1. The Monitor still cannot order anything. What changed is that its finding is admissible evidence in a proceeding a citizen can bring, and that the citizen has two distinct things to ask for — everything, or the parts that were never the secret. The second ask is usually the one that succeeds, and it is the one that was previously impossible.

Secrecy here has an outer wall at thirty years that nothing can move. Inside that wall, it is not a single locked door. It is a set of specific claims about specific pages, each of which someone can make the government defend.
