The Part of Korda That Cannot Be a Footnote

The committee could reconcile the service records. The delegates must now decide what those records should mean for the people who live beside the lake.

Mara IsetThe Korda Press·KordaYear 13, Month 12

Jalen Mire arrived at the Convention carrying a petition that would have sent the lake corridor to a referendum on joining Kelvant. He now speaks of something less tidy than the corridor on the petition map: the northern districts whose routes, work and services face toward Kelvant, and the southern lake communities whose daily life remains bound to Korda's interior.

That is not yet a concession. It is, however, a different argument.

At a sitting devoted to the Joint Committee's preliminary compilation, Mire declined to treat the committee's north–south comparisons as a substitute for the canceled vote. The petition had earned its place in the constitutional process, he said; its supporters had not signed up merely to furnish evidence for a later committee. But a democratic claim, in his account, must have a meaningful answer. He did not insist that the same jurisdictional answer was necessarily appropriate for every district whose records the committee examined.

Tomas Rell heard the opening and refused to call it a settlement. He has spent the Convention warning that a corridor departure could leave the rest of Korda with maintenance obligations, weakened public services and no credible route toward Statehood. He did not retreat from those concerns. He asked instead whether a departure confined to the better-connected north could be evaluated without pretending that the remaining Territory would be unaffected.

Neither man proposed a line. That absence matters. A northern route serving Kelvant does not establish title to a bridge. A southern facility serving interior patients is not proof that every adjoining household wants the same political future. The committee's compilation marks unsupported cost allocations as unresolved for precisely this reason.

The lake is not a residual asset

Dessa Orin listened to the exchange and brought the discussion back to the southern shore. Residents there have spoken about ferry connections, relatives across the lake, recurring service arrangements and Indigenous relationships to the water that cannot be reconstructed from a freight schedule. A plan that leaves the southern lakefront inside Korda could preserve those ties. A plan that claims to do so must still show how access and continuing services would operate.

Orin remains an advocate for whole-Territory Statehood. She has not endorsed any split. But she framed the question for proponents more concretely than before: if some northern districts leave, will the remaining Territory still possess the institutional capacity to pursue the Statehood Audits it has already sought? She said a promise to revisit that question someday would not be an adequate answer.

Her concern is not a veto over a corridor resident's aspirations. It is a demand that a proposed resolution address the Territory outside the corridor as well as the territory inside it—the work for which this Convention was summoned.

What the paper cannot decide

The committee has published a common body of evidence and left its limitations in plain view. It has not recommended a split, endorsed whole-Territory Statehood, settled cost-sharing or certified a new border. Its co-chairs, Mire and Rell, may now use that evidence as delegates, but the committee cannot make the political choice in their place.

Delegates leaving the chamber described conversations about northern transport districts, southern lake access, public-facility obligations and a continuing Statehood path. Some of those conversations took place in rooms where Assembly Speaker Elin Thoss had been meeting members separately. No participant identified an agreement, and the Speaker has announced none.

The Convention has not received a final proposed resolution. It still has a constitutional period in which to produce one; the committee's work neither shortens nor extends that clock. If it produces nothing, both competing petitions lapse and Korda remains undivided.

The striking change is that those who began by saying the petitions could not be reconciled are now trying to describe what a reconciliation would have to protect. The difference between a conversation and a constitutional resolution remains considerable. For the first time, though, that difference has become the Convention's business rather than an excuse to postpone it.
