The Citizen

A records error becomes a constitutional question when a birthright citizen is told to naturalize before casting her first vote.

§5.1 Citizenship §5.1.a State and Territory Voting Citizenship §5.1.b Civic and Constitutional Proficiency §1.8 Democratic Participation §1.1 Right to Courts and Legal Remedy §11.2 NVS Properties and Voting Rights
How to read this story. This is a fictional illustration, not Torenthian canon. The document-verification process and the election-administration timetable below are imagined statutory practices, not constitutional deadlines. The constitutional distinction is between citizenship acquired by birth and citizenship acquired by naturalization.

In Year 9, Mara Sen was seventeen, three months from her eighteenth birthday, and looking forward to the first federal election in which she could vote. She had been born in the Republic. At the time of her birth, her mother was a legal resident; her father lived abroad. Her mother became a citizen years later. Mara assumed that the citizenship entry in her local population record was correct.

It was not. During a routine check before the electoral period, an administrator noticed that Mara's parents had not been citizens on her date of birth. The administrator changed her status to “eligible to apply for naturalization” and sent a standard notice: complete the residency qualification and civic and constitutional proficiency assessment before seeking federal voting access.

Which pathway applies?

§5.1 · Citizenship from birth
A legal-resident parent is enough, where the child was born within the Republic.

Section 5.1 sets out distinct routes to citizenship. A child is a citizen from birth when at least one parent is a citizen; when born within the Republic to a legal-resident parent; or when born within the Republic and otherwise stateless. Mara met the second rule on the day she was born. Her mother's later naturalization did not create Mara's citizenship and the absence of a citizen parent did not negate it.

The proficiency assessment under §5.1.b applies to naturalization pathways. Mara was not applying to naturalize. She was asking the administration to correct its recognition of citizenship that already existed.

Two jurisdictions, one vote

Mara had lived in one State as a child and moved to another for school. When the first office declined to correct her status before receiving confirmation from the second, she was advised to register in neither place until the matter was settled. Her advocate pointed to §5.1.a: every citizen is a voting citizen of exactly one State or Territory, and no law may leave a citizen without one. Applicable residence or domicile law determines which jurisdiction must recognize her, while statute resolves competing claims. An administrative disagreement is not permission to make citizenship disappear.

The Elections Panel's job was not to decide Mara's political views or grant a discretionary exception. Her eligibility dispute had to be resolved under the governing law, consistently with §1.8's protection against actions substantially reducing access to the franchise. If the relevant officials failed to provide an effective remedy, §1.1 preserved recourse to an independent court. Neither the story nor the Constitution assumes a court must use a particular statutory expedited registration form.

The correction

Administrative determination · Fictional statutory process
The birth and residency records decide the question.

On review, the civil registry confirmed the place of birth and located the contemporaneous legal-residency determination for Mara's mother. The citizenship entry was restored, with the erroneous intermediate designation retained in the agency's audit history according to its governing records law. The two States then applied their ordinary domicile rules to recognize one voting jurisdiction.

By the time Mara turned eighteen, she could access the federal electoral process on the same legal basis as any other citizen. She was not excused from a requirement: the requirement had never applied to her.

What the distinction protects

Citizenship at birth is not an award from a registrar, a reward for passing an assessment, or a benefit that can be withheld because an official misunderstands a parent's immigration history. The Republic may require proof of facts relevant to status. It cannot replace §5.1's birthright rule with naturalization conditions meant for a different route.

There is a second safeguard in the same Article. Even naturalized citizenship cannot be revoked as punishment. Under §5.1, revocation of naturalized citizenship requires a judicial finding, supported by clear and convincing evidence, of material fraud in the application; it cannot produce statelessness or strip another person of derivative citizenship previously acquired. None of those revocation rules authorizes an agency to erase Mara's citizenship by clerical correction.

Constitutional finding

Citizenship follows the constitutional conditions, not the convenience of a database. §5.1 supplies the substantive status. §5.1.a prevents a citizen from falling between voting jurisdictions. §1.8 protects participation, and §1.1 provides a judicial remedy when administration fails.
