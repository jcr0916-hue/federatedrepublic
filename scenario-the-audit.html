The Audit

Year 3. Velmora completes its second Statehood Audit on time. The Monitor findings supply the record; an affirmative legislative determination supplies the decision. An inconclusive vote and a missed deadline have different consequences.

§15.2 The Statehood Audit§15.2.a Legislative Statehood Audit Determinations§9.1.a The Legislative Monitor§9.1.c The Judicial Monitor§3.5 The Senate

Velmora opened its Statehood proceeding at the beginning of Year 1. All three Monitors published their portions of the first audit before the two-year qualification period ended. That completed the audit record. It did not decide whether Velmora had met the constitutional standard.

The Assembly considered the complete record and determined compliance by a majority of its full seated membership under §15.2.a. Velmora remained a Territory. The second audit had to be completed within two years after completion of the first audit determined compliant; the later date of the Assembly's vote did not restart that clock.

Evidence Is Not a Vote

Year 2 brought a warning from one Monitor about an administrative defect. Neither a single adverse finding nor a count of favorable findings determines an audit's result. The responsible chamber must assess the complete record and state its reasons. Velmora corrected the defect while its qualification period continued. A noncompliant determination would not by itself end the proceeding while that period remained open or another timely completed audit awaited determination.

§§15.2 and 15.2.a — Completion and Determination
All three findings complete an audit. An affirmative legislative decision determines compliance.

In Year 3 all three Monitors published their findings on the second audit before the deadline. The Assembly's first motion failed to obtain a majority of full seated membership. That was an inconclusive vote, not a determination of noncompliance. Its procedures brought the matter back, and a subsequent affirmative compliance decision met the required threshold.

That second qualifying determination made Velmora a State immediately by constitutional operation. No separate discretionary admission act or executive confirmation followed. The legislative determination was essential; after it, Statehood was automatic.

Two Senate seats arose immediately. The Elections Panel assigned them by public lot to two distinct existing Senate classes and administered a special election within ninety days. The first senators served the remainders of those class terms.

When a Chamber Does Nothing

The initially responsible chamber must decide within the statutory period, at most ninety days after receipt of the completed record. If it does not, authority transfers automatically and exclusively to the other chamber. The receiving chamber has its own statutory period, also at most ninety days. It uses the same threshold, calculated against its own seated membership. The first chamber cannot reclaim the decision.

If the receiving chamber also fails to decide, expedited judicial review can enforce its duty. The court cannot decide substantive compliance in its place. Silence supplies neither admission nor rejection.

What This Scenario Tests

The two-year periods govern completion of qualifying audit records. A timely completed audit remains eligible for legislative determination afterward; legislative delay does not invalidate it. A proceeding lapses only when its qualification period has expired and no timely completed audit remains awaiting determination that could satisfy it.

Monitor independence supplies evidence without turning an auditor into an admission authority. Legislative responsibility supplies an accountable decision without a power to evade it forever. Judicial enforcement protects the process without substituting a court's policy judgment.
