The Audit

Year 3. A Territory passes the Statehood Audit, with clean findings from all three Monitors. No political vote required. The pathway is automatic. What happens next, in what order, and who can stop it.

§15.2 The Statehood Audit§9.1.a Legislative Monitor§9.1.c Judicial Monitor§3.5 The Senate

The Territory of Velmora had been working toward Statehood for four years. Its first Statehood Audit in Year 1 found material failures in two Monitor domains: unlawful legislative electoral arrangements in the LM’s findings and Article I non-compliance in criminal procedure in the JM’s. Those two findings produced an overall failed audit. Velmora published a remediation plan and spent fourteen months addressing them.

The second audit was completed in Year 3. Each Monitor examined its constitutional domain: the LM assessed legislative compliance, the EM executive and administrative capacity, and the JM rule of law and Article I compliance. All three published clean findings to the NRS. An overall failed audit would have required material-failure findings from at least two Monitors; a single such finding would instead have required remediation as a warning. Velmora passed.

§15.2 — What Is Automatic
No political vote is required once the audit passes. The pathway is automatic.

Passing the Statehood Audit is a constitutional trigger. Velmora became a State immediately under §15.2, without a political vote, executive confirmation, legislative act, or Provisional stage. The change was recorded to the NRS as soon as possible. Provisional status under §15.1.a applies only to an existing State on the remediation track.

Velmora’s government, courts, and administrative functions continued. Assembly representation remained proportional. Statehood immediately created two Senate seats; annual Statehood Audits became a continuing obligation.

§15.2 — Clean Completion
Immediate Statehood, followed by a special Senate election within 90 days.

The Elections Panel assigned Velmora’s two new Senate seats by public lot to two distinct existing Senate classes and administered a special election within 90 days. The first senators served the remaining portions of their assigned class terms. Existing States’ classes and terms were unaffected. Years 4 and 5 brought the same annual audit obligations that apply to every State; they were not a waiting period for admission.

The Legislature could not impose an additional admission vote, and an executive could not delay Statehood for political reasons. Judicial review remained available for legality, process, jurisdiction, and application of the stated audit criteria, without substituting a court’s policy judgment for a Monitor’s domain assessment.

The significance is not just procedural. It means that a Territory cannot be denied Statehood by political opposition once it meets the constitutional criteria — and it means that meeting the criteria is the only path. There is no shortcut and no veto.
