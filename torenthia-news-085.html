Asked About Korda, One Candidate Had Something to Send

Until this week, none of the four Legat Consul candidates had gone on record specifically about the Korda Convention. Asked directly, they did not answer alike.

Petra Vend Political Correspondent · Verentum Year 13, Month 12

The Legat Consul race has spent months on Lake Varda, Sunderland, and the Toren River. This week, for the first time, all four candidates were asked a version of the same question: what, if anything, should the federal government's posture be toward the Korda Territory Convention now underway.

Pell Sandris answered with a document. His campaign sent this paper an eleven-page memorandum on transition costs, court-seat relocation, and fiscal-transfer mechanics under either a whole-Territory or corridor-merger outcome — material his office said had been in preparation "since before the Convention had a committee to hand it to." Sandris has built his campaign on unusually granular Korda policy work before; this week's memo reads as an extension of that record rather than a new position. Asked whether he was trying to influence the Convention's committee, his office said the memo was "public information a legislature might use, not instructions for a body that doesn't answer to us."

Tobias Vael gave four sentences. "Korda is a domestic constitutional process. It will resolve the way domestic constitutional processes resolve — inside the room built for it. My attention is on the border and the water. I intend to keep it there." Pressed on whether a Convention outcome could affect national fiscal or judicial policy, his office declined to elaborate further.

Seren Mak was the most willing to characterize what the new committee means. "When a body starts forming committees instead of taking votes, that usually tells you who's losing the room," she said, framing this week's 27–6 vote to create the Joint Committee on Transition Facts as evidence of stalemate rather than what the Convention's own filing describes — an advisory, fact-gathering body with no binding effect on any eventual resolution. Asked to square her comment with the committee's stated mandate, her campaign did not respond by deadline.

Ines Carrow declined to comment on Korda specifically, citing the same institutional line her office has held throughout the race. "Territories and Fiscal Affairs is not my domain, sitting or running," a spokesperson said. "I'll answer for Foreign Affairs before and after this election. I won't answer for a committee I have no relationship to."

None of the four candidates' comments carry any effect on the Convention itself, which continues to operate under its own ninety-day period regardless of what any candidate for a different office says about it. Whether voters will treat granular preparedness, deliberate distance, pointed skepticism, or careful silence as the more electable posture remains, like the Convention's eventual resolution, an open question.
