Scenario · §2.16 · The Executive · Incapacity

The Twenty-Four Hours

The Council of Ministers determines that the Civic Consul is temporarily unable to exercise authority. He restores himself once after twenty-four hours. When the same incapacity recurs inside ninety days, the Council must reach two-thirds, restoration requires broader agreement, and the SC becomes available to police misuse.

§2.16 Executive Incapacity §2.16(2)–(5) Restoration & Recurring Incapacity §2.16(7) Declination §2.5(6) Acting Succession §2.6 Constructive Removal §10.1 NRS Permanence

Every constitution has to answer a question it would rather not: what happens when the person holding an office cannot do the job, and will not say so?

Most answers are bad. Give the decision to doctors and you have made a medical board into a constitutional organ. Give it to a court and you have asked judges to rule on a mind. Give it to the legislature and you have built a second removal power with a lower threshold than the real one, which is to say you have built a coup with a paperwork requirement. Give it to nobody and the Republic is run for six weeks by a man who cannot remember the meeting.

§2.16 refuses all four. Its answer is stranger and it is worth watching work.

The Setup
Nobody in this scenario is lying, and nobody is certain.

Civic Consul Aurel Vasko is fifty-eight. On a Tuesday in the ninth month he is forty minutes late to a Council session, arrives without the folder, and asks the Minister for Transport a question she answered on the previous Thursday. It is not dramatic. It would not be worth a paragraph if it had happened once.

It has now happened, in some form, four times in eleven days. Twice in private. Once in front of eleven people. Once, badly, in a corridor with a journalist in it who did not write it up because she was not sure what she had seen.

Vasko says he is tired. He may well be tired. He is fifty-eight, the Varda file has been on his desk since the spring, and he has not had a week off in two years. Everything he says about it is plausible and none of it is checkable, because tired and something else look identical from across a table.

If fewer than five Council members were available, legislators selected by lot would fill the deciding body to five — Senators for a Civic Consul proceeding, Assembly members for a Legat Consul proceeding. Here the Council of Ministers has eleven members in office. Nine of them have now seen at least one of the four episodes. None of them is a doctor. All of them have to decide whether the domestic executive of the Republic is being exercised by a man who is exercising it.

Panel One

§2.16(1) — The Determination
The Council may determine incapacity by majority, with at least five participating.

They meet on the Thursday. The vote is seven to four.

Read the mechanics carefully. The Council makes the incapacity determination, but the determination gives the Council no executive authority. Upon publication, authority goes immediately to the successor already fixed by the Constitution. The Ministers are not seizing the office; they are triggering a succession rule written before any of them formed a view about Aurel Vasko.

Where does the power go? §2.5(6). The order of succession, untouched by the Council, decided in advance, by a document that had never heard of Aurel Vasko.

NRS-Y41-1187 · Determination of Incapacity · Council of Ministers
FILED: Year 41, Month 9, Day 14, 16:22
BY: Council of Ministers under §2.16(1)
MEMBERS AVAILABLE: 11    PARTICIPATING: 11    THRESHOLD: majority (6), minimum 5 participating — MET
VOTE: 7 – 4

DETERMINATION: The Council determines that the Civic Consul is temporarily unable to
exercise the authority of the office.

GROUNDS AS RECORDED: Four observed episodes of disorientation between Month 9 Day 3 and
Month 9 Day 14, particularised in the annex.

EFFECT: Acting authority vests by constitutional operation under §2.5(6) upon publication.
The Council receives no executive authority from the determination.

Notice what is not in that filing. No diagnosis. No medical annex. No physician's name. The Council did not determine that Aurel Vasko is ill — it has no competence to determine that and the Constitution never asks it to. It determined that he is unable to exercise authority, which is a question about an office, not a body, and which eleven people who sat in the room with him are better placed to answer than any specialist in Valdrath.

Panel Two

§2.16(7) — The Declination Chain
A person upon whom acting authority would fall may decline within 48 hours. The person last in the order may not.

Acting authority falls first on the Speaker of the Assembly, who does not want it. She is nine weeks from an election in a district she holds by four hundred votes, and the last thing she needs is to spend those weeks being the woman who took Vasko's chair. She publishes a declination on the Friday morning. It costs her nothing: a declination is irrevocable and does not prejudice the person's eligibility for any future succession event — the Constitution explicitly refuses to punish her for it.

It falls to the next officer designated by statute, who declines on the Friday evening for reasons he does not give and does not have to.

It falls to the third, who says nothing at all. Acting authority has already passed to him upon his predecessor’s declination. On Saturday at 16:22, 48 hours after the incapacity publication, his silence makes acceptance final. §2.16(7): a person who does not publish a declination within 48 hours is deemed to have accepted. There is no sequence of events in which the office is empty because nobody answered the phone.

And underneath all of it, §2.16(7), four words that make the whole chain a floor rather than a slope: the person last in the order of succession may not decline. An acting officer retains their legislative seat but cannot exercise legislative functions while acting; the chamber provides for those functions. The music cannot stop. Somebody is always holding the chair, whether they want it or not, because a Republic that lets its succession run out the bottom has not written a succession provision — it has written a queue.

Panel Three

§2.16(2) — The Restoration
An incapacity ends when the Consul publishes a restoration declaration to the NRS. Full authority returns 24 hours after publication.

On Monday morning Aurel Vasko publishes four sentences.

NRS-Y41-1194 · Restoration Declaration · The Civic Consul
FILED: Year 41, Month 9, Day 18, 09:05
BY: Aurel Vasko, Civic Consul

I declare that the incapacity determined under §2.16(1) has ended.

FULL AUTHORITY RETURNS: Year 41, Month 9, Day 19, 09:05 (24 hours after publication).

That is the entire procedure. No hearing. No court. No vote. No doctor. No permission. The man the Council found unable to exercise authority ends that finding by saying so, and on Tuesday morning at five past nine he is Civic Consul again with every power he had on the Wednesday before.

The first reaction to this is that it is absurd — that the provision hands the incapable man the key to his own cell. Sit with it a moment longer.

Who else could hold that key? A court, and you have asked judges to adjudicate a mind. A medical board, and you have made physicians a branch of government. His accountability chamber, and you have created a removal power that needs no successor named, no absolute majority of full seated membership, no constructive vote — a §2.6 with the safety off. Every alternative to the Consul decides is worse than the Consul decides, and most of them are worse in a way that is permanent.

And the twenty-four hours are not decoration. They are the transition interval between the restoration declaration and the return of authority. The acting government has a known endpoint, the public record has a timestamp, and the Republic does not switch executive authority in the same instant that one person files a sentence.

Panel Four

§2.16(3) — The Second Determination
The same grounds inside ninety days now require two-thirds.

On Day 26 it happens again, in a Council session, in front of everybody, and it is not subtle and it is not tiredness.

The Council votes ten to one. This time a bare majority would not be enough. Because the determination rests on the same or substantially similar grounds inside ninety days of restoration, §2.16(3) requires two-thirds of the deciding body. Ten to one clears it.

Aurel Vasko publishes a second restoration declaration on Day 27. Unlike the first declaration, it does not restore him by itself. For the next thirty days he needs a majority of the deciding body to approve restoration, or he may ask the Supreme Court to review the heightened determination.

NRS-Y41-1217 · Heightened Determination of Incapacity · Council of Ministers
FILED: Year 41, Month 10, Day 10, 14:31
BY: Council of Ministers under §2.16(3)
MEMBERS PARTICIPATING: 11    THRESHOLD: two-thirds (8) — MET
VOTE: 10 – 1

DETERMINATION: The Civic Consul is again temporarily unable to exercise the authority of the office on substantially similar grounds within 90 days of restoration.

EFFECT: Heightened restoration requirements apply for 30 days unless a longer period is authorized under §2.16(4).

Panel Five

§2.16(3)–(5) — The Dispute
Nobody gets the office merely by refusing to yield.

Vasko insists that he has recovered. The Council does not agree: only four members support his restoration declaration. Acting authority therefore continues.

He petitions the SC. The question is deliberately narrow. The Court is not asked whether Vasko is medically competent, whether it likes his judgment, or whether the Council would govern better. It asks whether §2.16 was lawfully invoked and whether the stated grounds concern an actual inability to exercise the authority of the office. Political or policy disagreement alone cannot do the work.

On this record — repeated observed disorientation, the second episode in the Council room itself, and a ten-to-one heightened determination — the Court leaves the determination in place.

Panel Six

§2.16(4) — Time Without Removal
Thirty days is the default. Longer temporary incapacity requires both chambers.

The Council expects recovery to take longer than a month. The Assembly and Senate may each, by simple majority, authorize a specified longer period, never beyond six months from the heightened determination.

Assume they authorize three months. That vote does not find Vasko incapable and it does not remove him. It authorizes time for the Council's two-thirds determination to remain operative. Throughout the period Vasko can still return by restoration declaration plus a Council majority, or return to the SC if the constitutional basis for the determination no longer exists.

If three months expire without a fresh two-thirds determination, the heightened restriction ends. The incapacity itself does not vanish at midnight; ordinary §2.16(2) restoration again governs. An unconscious Consul does not regain authority by the movement of a clock.

What the revised provision is actually for. §2.16 separates the office from the authority of the office. Aurel Vasko remains Civic Consul throughout. His term runs, his benefits and protections continue, and no vacancy exists. Someone else temporarily exercises his authority.

The first interruption is intentionally easy in both directions: a Council majority can protect continuity, and the Consul can ordinarily restore after twenty-four hours. Repetition changes the burden. The Council must reach two-thirds; the Consul then needs a Council majority for early restoration or may test the invocation before the SC.

The Legislature controls duration, not incapacity. Both chambers can authorize more than thirty days, up to six months, but neither chamber decides whether Vasko is unable to exercise authority. If the political system concludes that he should no longer hold office at all, §2.6 remains available and nothing in §2.16 blocks it.

Every material act remains attributable. The Council's votes, Vasko's declarations, any legislative extension, and the Court's judgment all enter the public constitutional record. The mechanism does not ask anyone to diagnose a mind. It asks each institution a narrower question and prevents any one of them from quietly turning temporary incapacity into removal.
