Morantine Asks the Formula to Count the Wait

Morantine has submitted request-aging and service-load records to the reopened equalization review. Its proposal brings the years before funding into an argument the current formula does not settle.

Dara Voss RNN ·Verentum Year 13, Month 12

Morantine wants the years a capacity request spends waiting for funding to count when the Republic decides how to distribute that funding. It has now put records behind the request.

The territorial administration's submission to the Joint Committee on Fiscal Equalization, entered as NRS-Y13-0712, brings together the age of pending requests and the continuing demands on public health, transport maintenance and water infrastructure. Morantine asks the committee to consider writing request age and sustained service burden expressly into the statutory formula.

For a review that spent months without another session, that is a more specific argument to have. The committee has reopened its work under Chair Renata Kell's Month 12 notice. Morantine has supplied a proposed basis for change. The committee has not agreed to it.

The years before “funded”

The starting point remains the Executive Monitor's Year 13 certification. Reya Solt found that distributions followed the formula the Legislature had enacted. She also published the interval data the committee had requested: the median Territory request-to-funding time had risen from 2.1 years in Year 10 to 4.4 years in Year 13. For disadvantaged States, the corresponding figures were 1.8 and 2.0 years.

Those are figures for the groups in the certification, not a newly established Morantine average. The finding also explained the limitation in the formula: a request funded after two years and one funded after six are both recorded as funded.

Morantine's schedules give the review a way to look behind that final entry. They cover requests still pending and requests resolved during the review period, identify those outstanding for more than three years, and separate proposed capital work from recurring service expenditure. The service-load figures use the same reporting periods as the Monitor's interval data.

That separation matters to the practical question. A water project may require a capital allocation once. Keeping the existing system operating is a continuing expense. A delayed project and the services maintained while it waits are related pressures, but they are not interchangeable entries in an account. Any comparison that simply adds them together would need to explain what period and what work the total represents.

What should age be worth?

Giving waiting time an explicit place in the formula would require choices beyond recognizing that the wait exists. How much weight should a long-pending request receive? How should it compare with a newer request serving a heavier immediate burden? What evidence would distinguish sustained pressure from a short increase in demand?

These are questions raised by Morantine's proposal, not tests the committee has adopted. The receipt records no agreed weights and no draft statutory language. It also supplies no committee estimate of how much any jurisdiction would gain under a revised formula.

The reconvening notice leaves the review broader than Morantine's preferred factors. It includes infrastructure backlog and other measurable capacity pressures, and permits further State and Territory submissions. The committee may seek more evidence, recommend legislation or recommend no amendment. A place in its record gives Morantine's case something concrete to be compared with; it does not give it priority over the next submission.

The finding still stands

Morantine has not asked the committee to treat the Monitor's certification as withdrawn. Its submission does not allege that the mechanism is constitutionally inoperative. The argument is that the Legislature should consider changing what its formula measures.

Under §12.6, the Legislature must maintain a statutory mechanism ensuring reasonably sufficient fiscal capacity for every State and Territory to meet the specified social obligations and rights. An Executive Monitor failure finding would require a published legislative response within ninety days. The current certification made no such finding, so that response period is not running. Ordinary legislative review remains available without it; an amendment would still need to pass through the ordinary legislative process.

That distinction also preserves the political choice Elin Thoss made when she sent the formula toward legislative review instead of quietly moving favored requests up the queue. A general rule could give waiting time a weight that outlasts the administration applying it. Which rule, and at whose expense, remains for the legislative argument.

When RNN last reported on the stalled committee, the widening interval was on the record and the committee had not returned to it. There is now an open review and a territorial submission addressing what that interval should mean. Morantine's requests have not been funded by the act of filing. Its case for changing how they are assessed is now before the committee.
