Field Sheets and Floodwater

The Kessel culvert survey's first measurements are in Morantine's public works register. On the Valedon road outside Tessom, a measurement is not yet a repair.

{{ worldAuthor | escape }} {{ worldDate | worldDateLabel }}

TESSOM, Morantine — Now that the first field measurements from the Kessel culvert survey are in the public works register, the first thing you notice at the crossing is how little it looks like progress. There is survey ribbon on the fence posts, a chalk mark on the road shoulder, and the same dip in the Valedon road that floods every third spring. The road is open. No one has tendered a construction contract. There is only a record, newly entered, saying that somebody has finally measured the problem.

That distinction matters to Yelka Sarn, who runs Sarn Supply on the Valedon road and wrote in a Dispatch last year that the culvert was more than an inconvenience. She set the money in her till, which the Republic keeps honest, beside the water on her road, which nobody has kept off it: one promise she can feel in her hand, the other “a letter that quotes itself.” Standing by the crossing this week, she was not ready to call the survey a fix.

“Field sheets don’t make the water go down,” she said. “They’re better than the old form letter, I’ll give them that. But a measurement isn’t a culvert. I’ll believe it when a feed truck gets through here next spring without a detour.”

Tova Ferrin, a survey coordinator in the territorial works office, chose her words more carefully. The sheets, she said, record what the field crews have done: the dimensions of the drainage channel, the position of the inspection access, and the places that will need measuring again after the next scheduled rainfall. The office has asked for a cross-check against older maintenance drawings.

“The register doesn’t tender a contract, and it doesn’t make the culvert any bigger,” Ferrin said. “It’s paperwork about water. That’s what it says, and that’s all it says.”

Her caution matches the record. When the Territorial Authority commissioned the survey last month, its notice said the study commits no reconstruction funding and changes no pending capacity request. The register adds that no construction contract has been tendered and that the sheets are preliminary, not an engineering recommendation.

The culvert also sits inside a longer paper trail. Last year Morantine filed a written submission to the federal fiscal equalization review, with schedules of capacity requests outstanding for more than three years and service-load figures that include transport maintenance. The published receipt names no single crossing. It placed the material in the Joint Committee’s record, and the committee has not adopted Morantine’s proposed factors, assigned them weights, drafted statutory language or decided that an amendment is needed. No ninety-day response period is running.

In Tessom terms, the culvert is still near the bottom of a ladder whose top Sarn says she cannot see. The survey is a new rung. It is not the top.

“The money in my till is a protection I can hold,” she said, kicking a stone loose from the shoulder. “This culvert is field sheets in a register. I’m glad somebody’s measuring. I’m still waiting for the water to stop.”

Ferrin gave no date for the rainfall measurements, for the end of the survey or for its findings, which the Territorial Authority has said will be published to the NRS when the survey is complete. “When we have numbers, you’ll see them,” she said. “Slowly, and in writing.”

The ribbon is still on the fence posts.
