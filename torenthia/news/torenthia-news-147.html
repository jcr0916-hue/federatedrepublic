A Fence Below the High-Water Mark

A resident's public-trust suit over a shoreline fence in Lendower turns on where the high-water mark sits; the court has set no hearing.

{{ worldAuthor | escape }} {{ worldDate | worldDateLabel }}

LENDOWER — The fence runs about sixty yards along the shore, galvanized posts and wire, with a gate that has been closed since before the first snow. Tove Merral says it is new this season and wrong. She has filed a public-trust action under State §1.14(d) of Rhovane’s Constitution, which allows any person to enforce the Lake Varda public trust without showing individualized harm. Her complaint says the fence crosses shoreline below the ordinary high-water mark and blocks the route she has used for years to reach a family landing and watch waterbirds over the marsh.

Northline Moorage, which operates the adjacent docking and winter boat storage, says the fence is above the ordinary high-water mark on the line its surveyor supplied. The company says the gate is part of required safety improvements after ice damaged equipment last winter, and that a marked alternate path leads around the enclosed stretch to the public beach.

The dispute is narrow and unresolved: where the ordinary high-water mark sits on a stretch of upper-lake shore in the town of Lendower. The State’s Constitution treats Lake Varda as a public trust: water, bed, navigable channels, and shoreline below the ordinary high-water mark are held for present and future generations. Harbors and concessions can be operated privately, yet the trust may not be surrendered in a way that defeats navigation, reasonable access, water quality, ecological continuity, or long-term public use. State §6.3(b) allows private operation under lawful arrangements while saying essential public access and navigation shall not be permanently surrendered to private control.

At the county records office, a clerk made available Northline’s permit file, which has been on record since the summer. Attached to it is a surveyor’s certificate setting the company’s line. Merral’s filings dispute the certificate, arguing the line was drawn at a season of low water and does not account for winter ice and the shoreline’s gradual slope.

Hesper Dunmore, a Lendower resident, said the standing rule matters as much as the line. “Any person can bring this. You don’t have to own the property next to the lake or prove you lost money. That is what the public trust is for.” Dunmore also cautioned against reading the case as a demand that every point of shoreline always be open. A harbor-concession case decided in Year 8, Month 4 preserved reasonable access without forbidding private operation or asking judges to design engineering details, he noted; the law leaves room for working docks and winter storage.

The court has entered the matter on its docket. No hearing date has been set, and neither side has asked for an immediate order. The fence posts currently stand in frozen sand, and the gate has a notice directing walkers to the alternate path. Merral said she has been using that path, too. “I just want the line to be honest. If the line is above the water, fine. But nobody should put a gate on the lake and call it private without showing the line.”

Northline declined to comment beyond its filed response, which asks the court to dismiss on the ground that the fence is above the high-water mark and that the alternate path preserves access.

Until a hearing is scheduled, both sides are preparing. Merral said she has been photographing the shoreline after wind and ice events. The company has said it will provide maintenance records for the damaged equipment. Survey markers stand under snow between the fence and the water.
