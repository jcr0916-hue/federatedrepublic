The Tables Before the Ledger

With the fiscal equalization review still open, the civil servants assembling the civic-domain budget have to build on an assumption they can defend, and say plainly that it is one.

VERENTUM — The Council of Ministers met this month and, in the ordinary way, reviewed the statutory budget-preparation calendar. The published summary of that session is careful to the point of dryness. The discussion was informational, it authorized no expenditure, and it transferred no authority between the two Consuls. Each Consul still prepares and submits the budget for their own domain. Nothing in the record suggests a decision was made, because none was.

What the session does disclose, without meaning to, is a quiet pressure. Somewhere in the civic domain a budget has to be assembled that is defensible before it is generous, and the people assembling it are working against a number that has not been settled.

A federal budget is built on tables before it becomes a ledger. Expenditure is classified. Revenue is baselined. The cost of the social state, meaning healthcare, social assistance and the schools, is projected forward on assumptions that have to be written down and stood behind. A good budget office does not begin with the number a minister would like to see. It begins with what it can show, and lets the argument happen around that.

This year the hard part is fiscal equalization. The Joint Committee has directed its staff to prepare comparable illustrations of three approaches to the §12.6 mechanism: the present formula, a recurring service-burden factor, and a blended factor combining infrastructure backlog with request persistence. That direction was careful in the same way the Council’s summary was. No weights were chosen, no markup date was set, and no option was selected. The three illustrations remain drawings, not law.

A civic-domain budget cannot wait politely for the committee to finish. It has to be prepared on some assumption about what equalization will cost and return. The office preparing it therefore faces a choice that is not partisan, and is harder for that: pick an assumption it can defend, publish it as an assumption, and flag the rest as variance risk.

Adel Weyland is one of the career civil servants doing that work, not a minister and not a political appointee. The people who compile the tables are, by design, the people whose names do not lead. Weyland’s job, as colleagues describe it, is to be the one who says the sentence nobody wants said: a number that looks good in a brief is not the same as a number the office can defend to the Assembly.

The pressure on the other side is ordinary too. Every budget tempts its authors to look generous, and a spring in which voters are expected to choose a new Legat Consul makes every federal number a little more public. Against that sits the discipline Weyland represents: a table, once published, can be checked. Each Consul’s submission is published to the National Record System when it goes to the Assembly, and after a budget is enacted the Monetary Authority publishes its assessment of the likely effects on the economy and on fiscal sustainability. An assumption that cannot be shown will be found out, in public.

None of this means a submission has been filed or is about to be. The Council’s session made no decision, and no submission deadline has been published. It means only that the work of making the numbers honest is already under way, and that this year it has to be done around a blank space where a formula ought to be.
