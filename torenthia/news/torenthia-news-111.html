The Republic · Fiscal Equalization

What the Formula Can Measure

The committee has three approaches before it. The figures that eventually decide between them will not be politically neutral merely because they arrive in a spreadsheet.

Dara VossRepublic News Network·VerentumYear 14, Month 1

It is possible to agree that public services cost money and disagree completely about what constitutes an exceptional burden. That is the argument awaiting the fiscal equalization committee as the Republic enters Year 14.

The committee's published options memorandum does not select a reform. It compares retaining the present formula with standardized queue-age reporting, adding a recurring service-burden factor, and combining infrastructure backlog with the persistence of service requests. Each proposal is an attempt to describe pressure. None is yet a demonstration that money would follow it fairly.

A queue is evidence, not an invoice

A longer queue may expose a shortage of administrative capacity. It may also reflect a sudden influx of requests, inconsistent reporting, or an office that has become better at recording unmet demand. Publishing queue ages makes the problem visible; it does not by itself explain its cause. Keeping the existing formula while improving transparency would be the narrowest intervention, and for that reason the most defensible to those who want better evidence before changing distributions.

A recurring service-burden factor asks a more ambitious question: what is the continuing cost of serving people under conditions the present formula does not adequately recognize? Its difficulty is separating structural burden from inefficiency without assuming that every expensive service is poorly run or that every low-cost service is adequate.

The blended approach adds infrastructure backlog and persistent requests. It may capture pressure that annual expenditure misses. But a formula that rewards backlog without examining how it accumulated risks treating delayed maintenance as a permanent claim on other jurisdictions.

What a comparison must show

The useful next publication would not be a declaration that one formula is compassionate and another austere. It would be a distributional comparison: which jurisdictions gain, which lose, what evidence qualifies as a recurring need, and how officials could challenge an inaccurate count. Even then, estimates would remain estimates until legislators decided what tradeoffs they were prepared to defend.

The Executive Monitor has not declared a constitutional failure. There is no active emergency remedy clock under §12.6. A committee may study and propose ordinary legislation without being ordered by a constitutional finding to produce one result. That freedom is both an opportunity and a responsibility: if the eventual text changes the allocation of money, its sponsors must explain why.

For the moment, the most consequential distinction is between measuring a problem and paying to solve it. The committee has published ways to do the first. The second remains a political decision, still to be made in public.
