The Questions Candidates Would Rather Answer Later

An election can change who leads the Republic. It cannot move a county road into federal jurisdiction by applause alone.

Aleth FennThe TorenthianYear 14, Month 1

In an election season, a question becomes a slogan with remarkable speed. The Elections Panel has now offered the public a slower alternative: a register in which questions to the declared Legat Consul candidates may be published alongside any written answers the candidates choose to give. The register is not a debate stage. It records who was asked, what was asked and whether a reply arrived.

Ines Carrow, Seren Mak, Tobias Vael and Pell Sandris have filed declarations for the spring election. Their qualifications remain under review. The Panel has certified neither a ballot order nor a day for voting. That has not prevented the country from testing the difference between an appealing promise and a promise the officeholder could actually keep.

Consider Dorven. Orath's transport office has opened the tables behind its agricultural freight estimates for public inspection, including a log for corrections. County carriers can now challenge an estimate by pointing to a source entry instead of a speech. The office has authorized no new construction or spending. In a district Assembly race, Renna Ostrow, Dev Marrick and Fenwick Lund are each being pressed about the same roads, though an Assembly member, a county engineer and a freight dispatcher understand responsibility from very different desks.

Across the Republic, Varek's counties have submitted questions about Toren River maintenance. A Legat Consul may represent the Republic in dealings with Caldris; that does not give the office the ability to approve a county repair or manufacture funds for a State appropriation. Candidates can explain how they would negotiate. County councils must still explain what they will maintain.

It would be easy to call these distinctions tedious. They are, instead, the difference between a voter's instruction and a voter's wish. One candidate might promise a stronger national hand; another might promise patience with local authority. Until those claims are written down against particular decisions, neither tells a Dorven carrier when a bridge will be inspected.

The Panel's register may fill with polished answers or with blank spaces. Both are facts that can be reported without guessing at motive. Meanwhile a more useful political habit is taking shape: ask each candidate not only what should happen, but which institution would be legally responsible for making it happen.

The spring vote has not yet been scheduled to a particular day. There is time to ask.
