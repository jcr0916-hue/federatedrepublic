Put the Ledger Before the Referendum

A right-of-center columnist argues that Korda's affected voters should see the costed, enforceable terms of the Northern Lake Settlement before they are asked to ratify it.

Opinion

Selden Marek Opinion columnist · The Meridian Standard Year 14, Month 2

The Korda Convention has done its hard work. By a vote of 28–5 among thirty-three delegates, it adopted the Northern Lake Settlement: a proposed transfer of the three certified northern corridor districts, LC-N1, LC-N2 and LC-N3, while the southern lake districts and interior Korda remain part of the Territory. That is a serious compromise. Jalen Mire, Tomas Rell and Dessa Orin, who spent the Convention’s public life on different sides of this question, came together behind a resolution that keeps southern Lake Varda access with Korda and leaves the Territory’s Statehood path intact. We do not minimize what it took to get there.

Now the harder question begins. Under the Constitution, the adopted proposal must be ratified by the affected voters themselves, under the merger standard of 60 percent approval and 50 percent participation. Kelvant must approve through its own constitutional process. The Legislative and Judicial Monitors must jointly assess whether Kelvant’s constitutional integrity is maintained. The Senate must ratify by simple majority. None of those things has happened. No ratification date has been set. No electorate has been certified. Korda’s boundaries and status are unchanged.

That is not a defect in the process. It is the process. But it is exactly why this publication has a view about what should happen before the referendum is called.

The Settlement, as adopted, does two things at once. It draws a border, a narrow one, in the northern corridor. And it promises something: enforceable continuity of public services, and honest accounting for the title, contract and shared-cost questions that the record left expressly unresolved. The border is the part everyone can see. The promise is the part that will determine whether the border works.

We should be clear about what the record does and does not say. The unresolved items are unresolved, not hidden. The Joint Committee on Transition Facts was an advisory body; it compiled evidence and pointed to gaps without recommending a disposition. No one in the Convention has been accused of concealment, and we are not accusing anyone. The point is narrower and, in our view, more important: a promise that cannot be enforced is not a promise. It is a hope. And hope is a poor basis on which to ask a person to change which State governs them.

We are not arguing that the Settlement is legally incomplete. The Convention satisfied its constitutional clock, and nothing in the published record requires every shared-cost entry to be resolved before a referendum can proceed. That is the law, and we accept it.

But the law sets a floor. It does not set the standard citizens should expect of themselves and their leaders. Restraint is not delay. Order is not obstruction. Those of us on the right of center, who believe obligations should be honored, accounts should balance, and government should not ask people to sign for things it cannot deliver, ought to insist that the ratification campaign put the ledger before the referendum. Let voters see the costed, enforceable terms. Let them know what service continuity will require, who will pay for it, and what happens if the accounting does not close. Then ask them to decide.

We understand the instinct to move quickly. The Convention’s compromise is fragile, and delay can become its own kind of failure. But the fragility of a compromise is an argument for clarity, not for speed. If the Settlement’s terms are good enough to persuade the affected voters, they are good enough to be written down, priced and made binding. If they are not good enough for that, they are not good enough for a referendum.

The Meridian Standard is a new voice in the Republic’s public argument, and we intend to keep arguing from a consistent premise: the test of a good government is not the elegance of its intentions but the reliability of its commitments. The Northern Lake Settlement contains a real commitment. Before the affected voters are asked to ratify it, they should be able to see what it will actually cost, in money and in enforceable service terms. That is not an objection to the Settlement. It is the respect the Settlement is owed.
