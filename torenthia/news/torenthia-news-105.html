The Convention · Korda

The Votes No One Is Claiming

Three delegates are closer to describing the same settlement. They are not yet willing to claim the same result.

Mara IsetThe Korda Press·KordaYear 13, Month 12

A constitutional convention can move a long way without recording a single decisive vote.

Jalen Mire and Tomas Rell have signed a drafting memorandum. Dessa Orin has now answered it in a separate filing, setting out the conditions she would require before considering a northern-corridor settlement. There is still no adopted map, no Convention resolution and no affected-voter referendum. Yet each document has made the next one harder to evade.

The argument is no longer simply whether Korda should remain whole or whether the entire certified lake corridor should move to Kelvant. It is whether the Convention can define a northern departure that gives meaningful effect to the corridor's certified democratic claim while leaving southern communities, interior services and the Territory's path toward Statehood intact.

That question sounds shorter than the two petitions that started the process. It contains more work.

The price of a line

Orin's filing asks for a publicly defined retained lakefront, a lawful treatment of unresolved title and shared liabilities, identifiable responsibility for services during any transition, and a statement of how Korda's Statehood Audit path survives on revised boundaries. These are not decorations around an already agreed border. A line that fails those tests could strand a service institution or produce a remainder that has lost the capacity the Statehood petition sought to demonstrate.

For Mire, an equally difficult task remains. He cannot simply announce that petitioners who sought a vote on the certified corridor have received their answer because a smaller northern group might someday enter Kelvant. If he supports a narrowed proposal, he will have to explain what democratic effect it gives the original claim, why the excluded southern districts are treated differently, and what voters—not delegates alone—will still be asked to decide.

Rell faces his own constituents' harder question: how much departure can Korda bear? A northern-only adjustment may be less damaging than transfer of the entire lake corridor. That does not answer whether the remaining Territory can sustain its services and responsibilities without the former northern links. The Joint Committee documented existing obligations, not the final costs of changing them.

The Speaker's empty chair at the microphone

Elin Thoss has made no public case for a specific boundary. She is nevertheless a familiar figure in the corridors after sittings, listening to delegates whose concerns differ substantially: uninterrupted transport schedules, a Statehood process that does not reset into uncertainty, and certainty that a ratified settlement would still have to pass through Kelvant and the Senate.

Several members who had dismissed any adjustment now distinguish a northern departure from an entire-corridor transfer. Several who had demanded the original corridor referendum in full now speak about protecting a certified claim rather than one unchanged boundary. These shifts are visible, but they are not a signed coalition. No one in the chamber has released a credible final count.

In one exchange, a delegate sympathetic to a compromise asked how long the same unresolved shared-cost entries could remain in an implementing agreement. Another asked whether a favorable Convention vote would improperly be reported as Statehood. The answers were constitutional rather than political: the first problem must be addressed through enforceable transition procedures, and the second cannot happen because Statehood remains governed by §15.2.

Those questions tell us something about the room. Delegates are beginning to interrogate how an agreement would work instead of only what it would symbolize.

The decision still has to be made

The Convention cannot replace the Constitution's approval requirements with a good negotiating story. Any proposed resolution must address all affected portions together. If adopted, it must go to the affected voters and through the additional legal steps appropriate to its form. If no proposed resolution is produced within the constitutional period, the petitions lapse together and Korda remains undivided.

For the next sitting, delegates expect to work from the joint memorandum, Orin's conditions and the committee's common evidentiary register. They still need a boundary precise enough to submit to voters and a transition framework that does not invent figures the records cannot support. No Convention vote has been scheduled publicly as the final decision, and no delegate has claimed that an outcome is assured.

This is closer to a decision than Korda has been in months. It is also the moment at which a good phrase will no longer suffice. Soon the delegates must write down the line, name who bears the obligations, and ask whether enough people can live with both.
