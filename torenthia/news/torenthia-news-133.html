The Table Across the Lake

As the Valedon navigation conference opens, Korda shore residents weigh the practical uncertainties facing local lake traffic.

The Korda Press · Year 14, Month 2 · Sela Breck

By Sela Breck, The Korda Press — Proposed Year 14, Month 2

The talks opened in Valedon last month with three governments at the table and, somewhere north of here, a room full of people deciding how boats should move and who should answer when a boat is in trouble. Korda had no delegation of its own. But the lake does not care which side of a border a boat puts out from, and neither, in the end, do the people on Korda's shore, who will live with whatever comes out of the room whether or not their name was ever read in it.

Casper Orlin has been running the Warden's Turn along the southern reach since before the conference was a rumor. He keeps a folded notice pinned inside the wheelhouse — the marine bulletin that tells him where the survey marks are set and which channels are open. It is a local document, maintained by people who know this end of the lake, and it has nothing to do with Valedon. "That paper has kept me off the rocks for years," he said. "The people in Valedon aren't going to improve it. They might replace it, and I'd like to know what with before I'm asked to like it."

That is the shape of the concern along the shore: not opposition to a meeting, but the gap between a negotiation and the working arrangements that already exist. Orlin's bulletin is one of those arrangements. So is the loose custom by which the eastern landings radio ahead when the weather turns, and the informal understanding that the Warden's Turn will stand by for a clinic run even when the fare will not cover the fuel. None of it is written in any treaty. Some of those arrangements might eventually be affected by what the three governments agree to. Others may continue exactly as they are.

Lena Farr sees the question from the passenger deck rather than the wheelhouse. She makes the trip to the consolidated clinic at Trellin every other month, and in the thaw season the launch is the only reliable way. "What I want from any of them," she said, meaning the delegations, "is that whoever is supposed to answer the radio still answers it." She paused, then added the thing most people on the shore know but do not always say: the reason the conference exists at all is that something went wrong on this lake, and the governments could not agree on what. "So I do not expect them to agree about us quickly."

What Farr would not say — and this paper will not pretend otherwise — is that anything is broken today. The launch runs. The radio is answered. The lake is dangerous in the ordinary way a lake is dangerous, and Korda's fishers and operators have managed that danger with the tools they have. Sunderland's absence from the Valedon table is a fact about the guest list, not evidence that the southern crossings are failing. It is, however, a fact worth holding up: the nearest shore to Korda's own is not in the room where the lake's future is being discussed.

That absence sits differently depending on who you ask. To Orlin it is a practical worry — coordination with the Sunderland side depends on working relationships among operators, whose formal arrangements are not established by the conference record. To Nella Voss, who weighs out fish at the Trellin stall and whose family has set nets on this lake longer than the Republic has kept records of it, the concern is older and quieter. "The lake was here before the maps," she said. "It will be here after the meeting. What I want to know is whether the people in that room remember that somebody lives on the south of it."

Voss is careful about how she says such things. She does not claim the talks are an insult, or that Korda should have been invited, or that any treaty will be imposed on her against her will. She says only what is defensible: that navigation and rescue on Lake Varda are not matters that begin at a northern table, that Korda's shore has its own accumulated knowledge of the water, and that an agreement made without hearing it risks being an agreement that must later be explained to the people who were never asked.

The conference has set no closing date and produced no agreement. The opening record registers three working subjects — civilian passage, rescue contact, risk reduction — and explicitly notes that no government has adopted another's account of the earlier incidents. For the southern shore, that is the honest version of the news: a process has begun, at a table across the lake, about the water Korda lives on. What it will produce, and whether it will ask the people who know this end of the lake what they think, is still an open question. The Warden's Turn will sail regardless. The question is whether the new arrangements, if any, will fit the boats and the shore as well as the old ones did.
