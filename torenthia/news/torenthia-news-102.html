The Committee Publishes a Record, Not a Map

The evidence is finally in one place. It still cannot tell the delegates which future to choose.

Mara IsetThe Korda Press·KordaYear 13, Month 12

The Joint Committee on Transition Facts has delivered what it promised: a preliminary compilation that Korda's delegates can read against the same source list.

It has not delivered a territorial settlement. In the document filed with the National Record System, the committee does not propose a boundary, rule on statehood, settle the disposition of a public facility, or decide how the certified corridor claim should be implemented.

That is not a failure of the committee's mandate. It is the point of the division of labor. The committee was established to reconcile evidence. The Convention was convened to make a constitutional choice.

Three geographies, one set of source classes

The compilation organizes what administrators actually know about the northern lake corridor, the southern lake districts, and interior Korda. Some northern services connect more naturally to Kelvant and northward transport. A greater portion of the southern lake districts' present service arrangements reaches back into the interior. Shared facilities and contracts cut across those distinctions.

None of that produces a line on a map. A road's present route does not dictate future sovereignty. An operating contract may be amended. A family connection across the lake is not a fiscal entry that can simply be charged to one side or the other.

But the evidence does change the argument. Claims that the entire certified corridor has the same practical relationship to its neighbors now require explanation. So do claims that every shared cost makes any territorial adjustment impossible.

The missing figures remain visible

The preliminary document lists outstanding title references, contract amendments and shared-cost allocations. It provides no final comparison of the cost of every possible settlement. The gaps have not disappeared because a report has been printed.

That means a delegate can challenge an assumption without arguing that the whole record is unknowable. A proposal that moves a facility would need to address the obligations tied to that facility. A proposal that leaves Korda intact would still have to explain how its residents' conflicting certified claims receive democratic effect.

The co-chairs have made the next disagreement possible

Jalen Mire and Tomas Rell took the committee's leadership together despite opposing starting positions. Mire has insisted that the lake-corridor petition cannot be treated as a political inconvenience to be erased. Rell has insisted that the people outside the corridor cannot be left with a weakened Territory whose future was decided elsewhere.

The document gives both men something they previously lacked: a record they helped assemble, one that neither side can plausibly claim was written to recommend its preferred conclusion. It does not require either man to accept the other's answer.

Dessa Orin still favors a viable whole-Territory path toward Statehood. Her question remains what happens to Korda's institutional capacity under any disposition the Convention chooses, including one that changes its geography.

Now the decision belongs to the Convention

The law provides no automatic compromise merely because the committee has finished a first compilation. Under §15.5.a, a proposed resolution must come from the Convention and still face ratification by affected voters and whatever further constitutional approvals its form requires.

If no resolution is produced in the constitutional period, the competing petitions lapse together and Korda remains undivided. The committee's work has not changed that rule or paused the clock.

There is now less room to argue that the Convention must first await an organized account of the facts. There is still ample room to disagree about what the facts demand.

The next serious document will have to contain choices rather than another inventory.
