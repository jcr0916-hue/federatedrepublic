The Tables Before the Promises

The equalization committee has asked for corrections before publishing comparisons. For Korda and Morantine, that leaves the election debate with a question nobody can yet price.

Petra VendThe TorenthianYear 14, Month 2

VERENTUM — In the Assembly's fiscal equalization debate, the most useful number this week may be the number no one has yet published.

The Joint Committee has asked administrations to inspect the source tables that will underlie its three promised comparisons. The new submission notice invites corrections to mismatched reporting periods, duplicated entries and service categories that different jurisdictions may have counted differently. It is not a distributional forecast. It is the work that must happen before one can be trusted.

That distinction is inconvenient for politicians. Korda's backlog can be measured in roads, clinics and requests that have waited years for a response. Morantine's argument is that recurring service costs and aging facilities cannot be read fairly from the same thin ledger as an ordinary annual expense. Both positions are politically intelligible. Neither tells an Assembly member how a revised formula would redistribute a fixed appropriation until the comparisons appear.

A choice between definitions

Three approaches remain under consideration: publish clearer information about the age of outstanding requests, account more directly for the cost of delivering services, or recognize accumulated work left undone. Each answers a different question. Better queue reporting can reveal delay without paying to remove it. A cost factor can account for expensive service delivery while demanding a difficult standard for comparison. A backlog factor can acknowledge deferred work while inviting argument about whose decisions allowed it to grow.

Committee members must still choose whether any of these should shape a statutory formula and how to weigh them. The same amount of federal money cannot become larger merely because a table has more columns.

The approaching Assembly election has made that uncertainty difficult to ignore. District contests will return members who may eventually vote on a bill, but a candidate cannot honestly promise a particular allocation that the Committee has not yet calculated. Nor does a stronger coalition for Civic Consul Elin Thoss substitute for the separate legislative votes and appropriations that reform requires.

What a correction can do

The Committee has promised to preserve the submitted corrections with any resulting revisions to its tables. That procedural detail matters more than it sounds. An administration that disagrees with an eventual estimate will be able to point to the source figure it challenged, and readers will be able to see whether the Committee accepted the correction or merely recorded it.

For now the review has produced a new request for evidence rather than a settlement. The next useful headline will not be that a party has declared the formula fair. It will be that a member, and a voter, can finally see what each choice would cost and whom it would affect.
