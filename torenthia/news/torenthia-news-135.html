The Clock Is Not the Problem

An institutional columnist argues that the Speakers should prepare Executive Monitor succession deliberately even though no vacancy-triggered deadline is running.

Halen DruseOpinion columnist · The Verentum ReviewYear 14, Month 2

The Executive Monitor's office has an incumbent, and her name is Reya Solt. She holds the office today, and nothing in the record sets a date on which she leaves it. The pool from which a successor will eventually be chosen has been published and, by the two Monitors who audit it, found eligible. The two Speakers of the chambers have each recorded consultations, separately and by their own account, and no joint nominee has come of any of it. None of this is a crisis, and nothing in the law says it is one.

That last sentence is where the confusion usually begins, so let us be exact. The constitutional fallback — a public lottery to select a nominee if the Speakers do not jointly name one — begins upon a vacancy, within sixty days of it. No vacancy has been established, and no term-expiry date has been published. No clock is running. Anyone who tells you the Speakers are already in breach is wrong, and should be corrected without ceremony.

But the opposite error is just as common, and in its way more damaging: the assumption that because no clock is running, nothing is being measured. That is false. A scheduled succession is one of the few constitutional events that is knowable in advance, and the whole point of an appointment process that can be planned is that it should be. The drafters did not write "seamless succession" into the timing language (§9.3) as decoration. They wrote it as the standard the rest of the machinery exists to protect.

Here is the position this publication takes, stated so it cannot be misread: the lottery is a backstop, and a republic that drifts into its backstop through inaction has failed a test no statute was needed to administer. That is not a legal claim about a deadline. It is a governing claim about what deliberation is for. The Speakers are not late. They are choosing to use time, and the question the public should ask is what the time is producing — a joint nominee, or merely a longer record of separate consultations.

Let us also be precise about what the two paths actually are, because the law is clearer than the shorthand suggests. A candidate reached by the Speakers' joint nomination and a candidate reached by lottery are not different in their constitutional standing. Both are put to the same national popular confirmation; both, once confirmed, hold the same office with the same authority. §9.3 does not grade one route above the other. So the question here is not whether a lottery nominee would be a lesser Monitor. The question is whether the Republic will use its capacity to deliberate, or allow a lottery to do what deliberation was available to do better.

That distinction matters because of what a Monitor is. The Executive Monitor's authority is to observe, assess, and publish — an information mandate whose public confidence depends in part on how the selection was arrived at. A nominee produced by two elected Speakers who have actually negotiated is the product of an accountable act; a name produced by lot because no agreement was reached is the product of a process that had to be forced. Both are lawful, and both are equal in office. They are not equal in what they say about the governing class that produced them.

We do not know, and do not claim to know, which pool member either Speaker prefers. The record has published no preference, and we will not invent one. We do not know when Solt's term ends, and we will not imply that we do. What we can say, and will, is that the current posture — separate consultations recorded, nominee absent, no announced plan — is one that, if it is allowed to become a habit, is how a republic ends up choosing by lot rather than by negotiation. It need not end that way, and nothing in the record makes that ending inevitable. The Speakers hold the power to make it unthinkable. They should use it, not because a clock is forcing them, but because the standard they are meant to meet is higher than the deadline they are not yet under.

This column's view is firm. It is also, we trust, precise. The distinction between "no legal breach" and "no institutional failure" is the entire craft of governing well. The Speakers are not yet in breach. Whether they are failing is, for now, a question they alone can still answer in the other direction.
