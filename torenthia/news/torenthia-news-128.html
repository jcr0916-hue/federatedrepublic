Give Thoss the Votes to Govern

Keeping Thoss in office is a small ambition. Her supporters should want the votes to pass merit protections, settle the equalization fight and make government deliver.

Nella RuskOpinion columnist · The TorenthianYear 14, Month 2

Anyone telling you that Elin Thoss is safe because her opponents cannot agree on a replacement is selling you a very small ambition. Keeping the office is useful. Getting something done with it is why we ought to care.

I want our party to come back stronger this spring. I want Thoss to have members who will carry her program through the Assembly, and enough of them that every difficult vote does not become a negotiation over her survival. A claimant waiting for a decision cannot live on another session's worth of explanations. A northern town needs roofs.

The votes are still ahead of us. This is my judgment about what different results would mean, not a seat forecast. Start with the count we actually have: 266 members backing Thoss, down from the 274 who installed her. Those are coalition votes, not our party's seat total, and they are no guarantee on the next bill. Anyone who confuses the three will have a miserable election night and a worse first session.

If we make substantial gains and turn them into a dependable majority for legislation, I would put the civil-service merit proposal near the front of the queue. Thoss wants appointments and promotions protected from changes at the top. Good. We should want the person handling a claim to keep the job because they can do it, including the morning after our side loses power. If our reward for winning is a better chance to hand out jobs, we have misunderstood our own best argument.

A strong result would give our members room to insist on a serious statute rather than accept a declaration of good intentions to keep a wavering ally happy. Clearer rules for administrative discretion belong beside it. My test would be simple: can two people bringing the same problem to different counters expect the same answer? Thoss can identify the gaps. Assembly members must put the rules into law; she has no legislative vote of her own.

Equalization will test us harder. The committee has asked for comparable estimates, not chosen a formula. Korda's infrastructure evidence and Morantine's aging requests still have to be weighed. If that fight reaches the next session unfinished, a stronger party could help Thoss turn her demand for a report into a bill with votes behind it. If this Assembly finishes the law first, the next one will still have to fund and scrutinize its implementation.

Here is where a victory speech can come unstuck. A member may love Thoss until the distribution table shows less money for home. Recognizing recurring service costs is one choice; recognizing accumulated backlog is another; increasing the total pot costs money. We cannot promise every place a larger slice of an unchanged pie. A majority worth having would defend a common rule through the first angry local headline. A pile of members who each demand an exception would merely give Thoss more people to telephone.

Nor would a good Assembly result sweep away the Senate. She would go into those negotiations stronger, with a credible prospect of keeping her own side together. She would still need the other chamber. That is a reason to elect members who can negotiate a bill without surrendering its purpose.

Now suppose we fall short and the Assembly comes back in pieces. Thoss might survive quite comfortably while her program starves. Her opponents can agree that she ought to go without agreeing who ought to replace her. We should understand that distinction. We should be ashamed to use it as our definition of success.

I would expect merit reform to be narrowed in that chamber: perhaps enough votes for appointment protections, with the harder promotion provisions left for another round. Regulatory rules could advance one area at a time. On equalization, standardized queue reporting might attract members who will not yet support moving money. Those could be worthwhile bargains. Reporting the age of a request, however, will not pay for the service being requested. We would owe our readers an honest account of what each bargain left undone.

The small groups holding the deciding votes would know exactly what their support was worth. Thoss would have to find the overlap between their demands, and our party would have to decide which concessions preserved the reform and which emptied it. I would rather accept a narrower merit law with real protections than a grand title full of exemptions. I would rather see the cost of an equalization deal on the page than hear that the coalition had reached an understanding.

There is work she could keep doing: publishing the monthly claims figures she has promised and attacking backlogs within existing powers and appropriations. But do not ask an administrative instruction to do a budget's job. If bargaining produces a budget impasse, the Constitution keeps specified continuing functions funded at the prescribed level. It does not let her start or expand programs under that interim appropriation. Staying in office would offer cold comfort to people waiting for a government capable of doing more.

Then there is the result we ought to work to prevent. If our losses leave her opponents able to agree on a successor, the danger is real. Replacing her takes an absolute majority of the seated Assembly voting for the same named serving member: 251 votes if all 500 seats are filled, fewer if vacancies reduce the seated membership. An election defeat alone does not remove her. A successful constructive vote does. Until then she remains Civic Consul.

After that vote, someone else would choose the incoming ministers and prepare the civic budget proposal. Existing law would remain, and outgoing ministers would maintain existing policy until replaced. But a merit proposal still waiting for sponsors could lose its sponsor in the executive. Regulatory reform could slide down the list. Equalization could acquire a different champion with different priorities, or spend another session behind something else.

A successor might keep parts of her program. Some of these proposals ought to win support beyond our ranks. But we would have given other people the choice of what to pursue. A protection written into law is harder to lose than a proposal in Thoss's next-session folder. That is why our party should be trying to win the votes to enact things while it can.

And before we promise ourselves that a bigger caucus would settle Riverglow, look north. The housing act passed the Assembly 288–201; that was support for housing, not a new confidence count. Thoss sought to remove the siting language affecting land beside Argent Ridge. The Speaker's last published entry paused the review and put a withdrawal motion on the schedule. We still have no published outcome. We cannot put completed housing on our record yet.

Norvane has not consented to Trust designation. More homes could address the pressure behind its refusal; they would not purchase its consent. Protection still needs the host State and two-thirds of both chambers. The separate repeal campaign cannot supply that protection by itself. Anyone offering Riverglow as the automatic dividend of a Thoss majority is promising what even a strong ordinary majority cannot deliver.

Thoss has refused to turn her office into a campaign organization. I respect that. It leaves the political work to those of us who want her program to succeed. We can begin by asking our candidates what they will actually vote for, rather than accepting a warm sentence about competent government.

And take those questions into your own district. As this paper's election report explains, single-member districts use majority-based ranked choice; multi-member districts use proportional ranked choice. A national cheer for Thoss does not turn itself into seats. The members returned, and the commitments they are prepared to keep, will decide how much bargaining she must do.

I want our party to give her room to govern and then hold her to the use she makes of it. If we gain that room, pass the protections and defend the difficult funding choices. If we scrape through, tell us what was traded away. If we lose, do not measure success by how long she can hang on.

We are asking people to trust us with their government. We ought to want more for them than Thoss's survival.

Nella Rusk is an opinion columnist for The Torenthian and a supporter of Thoss's party.
