The Crew at Tessin Ford

At Tessin Ford, a county works crew begins the ordinary spring count of changes along the Toren River.

The Kellor Courant · Year 14, Month 2 · Maren Dael

TESSIN FORD — The first work of the season is not a repair. It is a count.

Tarn Evelle walks the Toren's near bank at first light for the better part of a month each spring, before the snowmelt has fully committed to its run, and he counts the things a river leaves behind over winter: the snags, the scoured gravel bars, the lengths of bank the ice has chewed back. Evelle is a works foreman for Hollowmere County, and the count is the quiet beginning of everything that follows.

"The river changes every winter," he said, walking a stretch where last year's shingle has been pushed twenty paces upstream. "You can't trust last spring's map. You have to look again."

Beside him, Dren Ostall, a seasonal hand in his third season, drives the first marker posts into the soft ground where the bank is beginning to undercut. The posts are not a repair. They mark a place to watch. Ostall's grandfather worked the same stretch, and he repeats the old rule as if it were scripture: mark it now, or pay for it later.

The work itself is unglamorous and unceasing. A crew clears the snags that would otherwise gather more debris and send a jam downstream. It grades the ford approaches where spring traffic turns the road to muck. It checks the culverts before the first heavy rain, because a blocked culvert in the thaw can become a washed-out road later. None of it is dramatic. All of it is the difference between a river that rises and a river that rises and takes something with it.

The people at Tessin Ford remember why they do this work. In Year 6, the flood took the ford crossing, and the evacuation and the emergency supplies and the short-lived orders that followed are still talked about here. But Evelle is careful to say that memory is not the same as alarm. "That was a flood," he said. "This is an ordinary spring. You prepare for the second, and you remember the first. You don't treat them as the same thing."

The frustration Evelle describes is not dramatic either. It is the ordinary gap between what a crew sees and what a piece of paper captures. His crew fills in tally sheets each week — work done, snags removed, culverts inspected — and sends them to the County. The sheets describe a stretch of riverbank. They do not describe what it feels like to stand on a bank that has moved under your feet, or which gravel bar is the one that will matter in a hard rain.

"Someone in an office can read that we cleared a stretch of bank," Evelle said. "They can't read exactly why we did that part first, or what we decided to leave alone because moving it now would make it worse."

That is the whole texture of the work at the ford: judgment that lives in the hands of the people who stand on the bank, only partly translated into records at the County works office. The crew does not resent the paperwork. It is the way the work is asked for and paid for. But Ostall put it plainly: "The river doesn't read our sheets."

By late morning the first count is done and the posts are in. The crew loads the wagon and turns for the county yard, leaving the Toren to its business. The melt has not yet peaked. The ford is still passable. Evelle will walk the bank again tomorrow, and the day after, counting the same things in a river that is never quite the same twice.

That is the job. It has no single chief, no single office, and no single season in which it is finished. It has only the people who do it, and the river that keeps them honest.
