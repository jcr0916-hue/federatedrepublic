Of Korda, For All of It
Statehood

Certified

The Elections Panel verified the signatures Tuesday. Ten thousand names, most of them from the interior, filed on behalf of the whole of Korda Territory — the lake corridor included.

Mara Iset The Korda Press · Adren, interior district Year 13, Month 11

The petition sat in a records office in Adren for four months before anyone outside these districts noticed it existed. It didn't need anyone to notice. It needed signatures, and it got them — enough that the Elections Panel's certification this week was a formality by the time it arrived, not a surprise.

Bern Calloway drove two hours each way to sign his in person, at the collection table outside the co-op in Adren, rather than authenticate through the NVS from home.

"I wanted to put my hand on the paper. Call me old about it. My grandfather waited on a road repair for eleven years and died before it got fixed. I wanted the signing to feel like something, not just a screen."

Calloway runs a grain operation forty minutes north of Adren. He is not, by his own description, a political man. He came to sign because the freight road serving three interior counties has needed resurfacing since before his children were born, and every capacity request filed on it has come back the same way — acknowledged, queued, unfunded.

That is the shape of most of the conversations at that table, from what the organizers say. Nobody at that table was signing to make a statement about Kelvant. They signed about roads, about a hospital in Dessin that's been asking for a second surgical suite for six years, about a school district that redraws its bus routes every autumn because the roads it needs don't exist yet.

What Certification Actually Does

It means Korda's case now goes to the same process every Territory-to-State petition goes through — the Statehood Audit, administered by the Joint Monitor Council, testing whether the Territory as a whole could stand as a State in its own right. Nothing about certification decides that question. It only means the question gets asked formally, on the record, instead of sitting in a records office where nobody outside Adren has to look at it.

The petition's own language covers the Territory in full, corridor included, and the Panel's certification does not carve any portion out of it. A separate petition affecting only the lake corridor was certified roughly five weeks earlier under a different provision. What relationship the two will have is not something this paper is equipped to resolve today.

Not Everyone Is Convinced It Matters

We asked a member of the corridor's own petition organizing committee for a reaction. The answer, paraphrased at the source's request, was that nobody in Adren asked the corridor whether it wanted to be included, that Statehood Audits take years even when they go well, and that "a petition signed inland doesn't speak for people who already cast their own."

Maybe that's right. Audits do take years. Whether it changes anything on the lake side is a question this paper isn't equipped to answer, and won't pretend to.

What we can report is that ten thousand people who have not, in living memory, had a petition of their own on file with the Elections Panel now do. Bern Calloway's signature is one of them. He says he isn't thinking about Kelvant. He's thinking about the road.
