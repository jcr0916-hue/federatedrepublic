Voluntary Devolution · §15.4 · State Referendum · Senate Ratification

The Petition

Year 7. A State that has been a net federal redistribution contributor initiates voluntary devolution — conversion from State to Territory status. The constitutional requirements, the negotiation, and what devolution actually means.

§15.4 Voluntary Devolution and Merger§15.5 Re-Qualification Pathway§3.5 The Senate§12.1 The Social State and Monetary Authority

Cassren had been a net contributor to federal redistribution for eleven consecutive years. Its legislature had calculated that its residents paid substantially more in federal taxes than the value of federal benefits and social state services they received. The majority coalition ran on a platform of fiscal autonomy. After winning, they moved to initiate voluntary devolution.

Voluntary devolution under §15.4 would convert Cassren from a State to a Territory within the Republic. Its restructuring agreement would address assets, liabilities, transition timing, successor status, fiscal obligations, and continuing obligations. Cassren retained full State status and Senate representation until the agreement took effect. The referendum authorized pursuit of devolution; a negotiated agreement still required Senate ratification.

§15.4 — The Requirements
Three steps, in sequence: a State referendum on the principle of devolving, negotiation of a restructuring agreement, and Senate ratification of that agreement.

The first step is the principle referendum. Cassren's legislature placed the question — should the State pursue conversion to Territory status — before the State's eligible voters. The ballot did not yet specify restructuring terms; under §15.4, negotiation only happens once the principle vote succeeds, so the State and the federal government aren't spending months negotiating fiscal arrangements and transition terms that voters might reject outright. The referendum achieved 64% approval on 71% turnout. The thresholds — 60% of votes cast and at least 50% participation — were met. No challenge was filed during the 30-day post-certification window, so negotiation could proceed.

Only then did negotiation begin. This produced the substantive document: how existing federal contracts are handled; what happens to the federal use rights on land within Cassren's territory; the transition timeline; continuing obligations on both sides; and the fiscal arrangements — including federal tax obligations — that would govern Cassren's relationship with the Republic going forward. Cassren and the Legislature negotiated for eighteen months. The agreement was published to the NRS. The final step was Senate ratification, requiring 2/3 of full seated membership — a high threshold that ensured the restructuring had genuine federal consensus, applied now to the actual negotiated terms rather than to a principle the voters had already decided.

§15.5 — What Comes Next
Cassren became a Territory on the effective date. Re-qualification began immediately.

On the agreement’s effective date Cassren became a Territory and ceased to hold Senate seats. It retained proportional voting Assembly representation, social-state protections, and Article I rights. The rule allowing sitting senators to finish their terms applies to Provisional States; it does not preserve Senate representation after a State has actually become a Territory.

Under §15.5 Cassren entered the §15.2 re-qualification pathway immediately when devolution took effect. No additional political vote was required to enter that pathway. A later passing Statehood Audit would restore Statehood immediately and create two Senate seats under §15.2; it would not revive an expired Senate term.

The federal government could not have blocked Cassren's petition once the referendum and Senate ratification thresholds were met. No executive veto applied. The constitution provides a path for a State that wants a different relationship with the Republic. Using it is the State's constitutional right.
