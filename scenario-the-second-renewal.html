The Changed Mission

Year 8. The Halvern Strait escorts are succeeding, but the Legat Consul now proposes a shore-based training mission. The Senate demands an updated plan. An expanded mission requires a new law and appropriations, while military command remains with the Legat Consul.

§12.2 Fiscal Accountability §14.1 Authorized Purposes for Military Force §14.2 Defense Authorization and Allocation §2.2 Military Authorization Framework

The Halvern Strait escorts were working. That was the difficulty. Armed raiders had struck vessels of Caldenmere registry and a treaty ally in Year 6; a law authorized convoy escort and interdiction to protect citizens and allies under §14.1. The Assembly appropriated the money. Legat Consul Maren Josk chose the routes, force mix and methods. Attacks fell by four-fifths in the first year. A statutory renewal passed the Senate 44–9 in Year 7.

The authorizing law used annual terms. The Constitution did not supply that calendar, a twelve-month ceiling or a special consequence for a second renewal. Its continuing demands were more practical: keep operations within their authorized purposes and scope; account for spending; report material changes; and obtain further legislative authorization when the mission outgrew the law.

The Mission Changes

By Year 8, Month 3, Josk's proposal was no longer just an escort operation. The littoral state's coast guard had collapsed. Keeping the strait open indefinitely would require Republic vessels indefinitely, unless somebody built a replacement. Her staff proposed a shore-based training mission, harbor works and a transfer of patrol responsibilities. Those were serious answers to the strategic problem. They were also activities outside the existing escort-and-interdiction authorization.

Josk could alter an escort route without asking the Senate. She could not turn authority to protect convoys into authority to establish a training presence ashore merely by calling it a different method. Under §14.2, a material change must be reported to the Senate; an expansion of authorized purposes or scope requires further authorization by law. The distinction between a command decision and a different mission became the argument.

§14.2 — Review and Command
The Senate can require an updated plan. The Legislature must authorize the larger mission.

The Military Affairs Committee asked for an updated operational plan. Josk objected to publishing a fixed departure date: the raiders could simply wait. The committee accepted that objection without accepting an indefinite blank cheque. It sought named responsibilities, evidence of patrol capacity, withdrawal arrangements and the cost of each stage. The Constitution required provision for orderly withdrawal and existing obligations; the particular capability thresholds were choices the institutions had to defend.

For eleven weeks Josk's staff worked with the committee on operational review and with the Assembly on authorizing language and appropriations. The Senate did not issue sailing orders. The Assembly did not acquire military command by voting the budget. Each institution had a decision it could make and a boundary it could not cross.

National Record System — Senate Review — Year 8, Month 7
UPDATED OPERATIONAL PLAN — Halvern Strait Escort Operation
Received and reviewed under §14.2

Proposed scope: convoy protection; a limited coast-guard training mission with the littoral government's agreement; harbor support; transfer of patrol responsibilities when specified capacity thresholds are met.

The plan provides for personnel protection, orderly withdrawal and fulfillment of lawful obligations. Detainee arrangements remain subject to Article I. The proposed final stage falls in Year 12; retaining forces afterward requires authority covering their continuing mission.

The Senate's review does not itself authorize the expanded mission or appropriate funds. The proposed expansion and its budget are submitted for legislation originating in the Assembly. Command within the eventual authorization remains with the Legat Consul.

The Law, and the Work

In Month 8 both chambers passed the revised authorization, including the agreed stages, and the Assembly-originated appropriation supplied the funds. The escorts sailed the next morning. The constitutional work was not the number of renewals: it was making an expanded commitment pass through the institutions that could lawfully authorize and pay for it.

The coast-guard mission stood up in Year 9. A first patrol squadron met the stated capability threshold in Year 10 and an escort element stood down. The Executive Monitor audited expenditure and published findings; it did not certify military strategy or order a withdrawal. Civilian harbor support required lawful administration in the Civic Consul's domain and appropriations through two budget cycles. A Senate-reviewed operational plan could not by itself bind that Consul to spend money the Legislature had not supplied.

In Year 12 the patrol capability was real but uneven. A fresh, narrower law retained one escort element for a stated §14.1 purpose and funded the remaining obligations. The Republic chose a smaller continuing commitment on the record. Neither the success of the old operation nor its treaty background supplied authority for every new task.

What This Scenario Tests

Operational judgment belongs to the Legat Consul within lawful purposes, scope and appropriations. Material changes bring Senate reporting and review; an expanded mission requires further legislative authorization. Review, authorization, appropriation and command are related powers exercised by different institutions.

If authorization or appropriations end, §14.2 terminates authority to pursue the operation's objectives. Orderly withdrawal, protection of withdrawing personnel and existing lawful obligations remain possible, subject to the Legislature's appropriation authority. There is no automatic ninety-day wind-down allowance.

A ratified treaty may authorize the action its terms require under §14.4. It does not dispense with operational review, appropriations or the limits of its own terms. Immediate defense under §2.2 is a separate authority; the Senate may require a return to alert posture when continued active defensive operations are no longer justified. None of those routes converts a familiar mission into unlimited permission.
