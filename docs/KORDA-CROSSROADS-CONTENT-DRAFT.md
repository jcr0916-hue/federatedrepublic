## Intro screen

### Title
**The Convention**

### Premise
Korda has two certified claims on the same ground and one room in which to reconcile them.

The lake corridor qualified a petition to join Kelvant. The referendum was scheduled. Then a second petition covering all of Korda qualified inside the same constitutional window. The Joint Monitor Council sent both claims into a Territory Convention instead. The corridor challenged that decision. The Supreme Court let it stand.

Now the litigation is over. The Convention has moved from Verentum into Korda. Thirty-three Assembly delegates have about eighty-seven days left to produce one proposed resolution addressing all affected portions together. If they fail, both petitions lapse.

You are one of those delegates.

You do not control the room. You do not control the clock. And even if you win inside the Convention, the voters affected by the resolution still get the final word before the ordinary constitutional process continues.

# Playable Role 1 — Sena Threll

## Picker card

**Sena Threll**  
Interior delegate · Adren region

You spent years routing grain around roads that were always on somebody else's funding list. You came to the Convention believing Korda should remain whole and become a State. The corridor's petition is lawful. You also think it would leave the rest of Korda weaker.

**Starting position:** Whole-Territory Statehood, no special corridor carve-out.

## Full role introduction

The first thing you learn in agricultural logistics is that maps lie.

A road can exist on a map and still be useless six weeks every year. A bridge can be open and still cost a cooperative half a day because the approach cannot take a loaded truck. A federal capacity plan can list your district for four years and still leave you calling three drivers at dawn because somebody has to get grain around the washout.

You did that work before anyone put your name on a ballot.

Dessa Orin did some of it too, later and from the Assembly. She learned how to get the problem into committee records. You learned how to get a shipment to market anyway. You respect her. Some days you also resent how easily a capital can turn years of other people's improvisation into one politician's good sentence.

The lake corridor has a different complaint. Its residents organized a petition to join Kelvant, cleared the threshold, survived the required assessment, and got a referendum date. Then the whole-Territory petition arrived and the Constitution forced both claims into this room. The corridor never got its vote.

You do not think that makes the corridor wrong.

You think it makes the choice dangerous.

If the corridor leaves, inland Korda loses freight, assessed economic activity, and the part of the Territory that received the investment everyone else was told would eventually spread outward. Your district would not have had a vote on the merger. It would have lived with the result.

So you arrive wanting one thing: Korda whole, Korda a State, and no bargain that solves the corridor's grievance by making the interior permanently dependent on it.

The problem is that wanting one thing and having seventeen other delegates want enough of it are not the same skill.

# Playable Role 2 — Davin Kesh

## Picker card

**Davin Kesh**  
Lake-corridor delegate · Varenne Station area

You helped collect signatures for the petition that was supposed to put the corridor's future to a vote. It certified, cleared review, and received a referendum date. Then another petition erased the ballot before anyone could find out whether you would win.

**Starting position:** Preserve corridor distinctness — negotiated merger if possible, entrenched autonomy at minimum.

## Full role introduction

You know exactly how many signatures fit in the metal case under the passenger seat of your old freight van.

You know because for three months you drove that case between depots, market yards, ferry approaches, and warehouse offices whenever the day's hauling left you an hour to spare. You were not the face of the corridor petition. You were one of the people who made sure there was a petition to have a face.

It certified.

The viability assessment cleared it.

The Elections Panel set the vote.

Then, before anyone cast a ballot, the whole of Korda qualified a second petition and the constitutional machinery changed tracks underneath you.

You did not lose a referendum.

That would almost be easier.

You lost the chance to find out.

People inland tell you they were facing a decision that would strip away too much of Korda's freight and tax base without giving them a vote. You understand the argument. You hauled enough inland freight to know those towns are not imaginary lines beyond the corridor. But your voters followed the process available to them. They signed. They waited. They cleared every gate. Now some of the same people who had no vote in the corridor referendum sit in a Convention empowered to decide what replaces it.

The Court says that is constitutional.

Constitutional is not the same thing as settled.

You arrive determined that the corridor will not disappear into a whole-Korda resolution as a footnote. A negotiated split remains your preferred answer. If that cannot command the room, you want structural protections strong enough that the corridor does not spend the next generation being told its distinct interests are merely local complaints.

Thirty-two other delegates have their own version of fairness.

You have eighty-seven days to make enough of them live with yours.

# Scene 1 — First Sitting in Korda

## Shared scene setup

The first sitting after relocation begins without ceremony.

The room is larger than the temporary chamber in Verentum and less polished. The press seats are full anyway. Residents line the back wall until staff stop adding chairs. Outside, people who spent weeks traveling to the capital to watch their own constitutional proceeding are discovering that proximity does not make the arguments simpler.

The clerk reads the Supreme Court disposition into the Convention record, then the relocation vote, then the calendar.

About eighty-seven days remain.

No one has filed a resolution.

That fact hangs in the room longer than the clerk's voice does.

Dessa Orin is three seats to your left. Jalen Mire, from the lake corridor, has a folder open in front of him with the old referendum notices still clipped inside. Tomas Rell has already filled half a page with names and arrows.

The chair asks for motions concerning the Convention's order of business.

This is the first moment since the Court ruling when nobody else has decided what happens next.

### Player prompt

**What do you do first?**

You can force the substantive question into the open, build a cross-bloc working group, spend the opening days counting and persuading quietly, or organize your own side before bargaining begins.

No option requires you to change what you want. The question is how you intend to move the room.

### `s1_threll_force`

**Provisional deltas:** interior +10 · corridor -8 · swing +2 · days -4 · trust 0

**Response prose:**

You stand before anyone can build a procedural wall around the morning.

"Put Statehood first."

There is an audible reaction from the corridor seats, but not surprise. Everyone in the room knew where you started.

You keep the motion narrower than some of your own allies wanted. No final vote today. No language pretending the corridor petition never existed. You ask only that the Convention make whole-Territory Statehood the first substantive proposal formally debated.

Rell seconds.

Mire does not.

Orin waits until the chair recognizes her and then asks the question that changes the temperature of the room: whether your motion is intended to establish priority or entitlement.

"Priority," you say.

It costs you nothing with the interior. If anything, delegates who worried the relocation would become weeks of process now have proof that somebody intends to use the clock.

The corridor hears something else: the first local sitting has barely opened and the interior is already trying to decide which claim gets to speak first.

The motion does not settle anything. It does what you wanted.

It makes everyone choose where to sit for the first argument.

You ask for six chairs.

The difference will matter only if you can keep it that way.

You do not begin with the corridor.

The first real negotiation happens with people who mostly agree with you.

It is not merger.

It is enough to make the interior notice that the corridor has stopped speaking in eight voices.

# Scene 1 balancing notes

These deltas are placeholders, not final numbers. The intended lessons are:

- **Force** strengthens the player's home bloc quickly, sharpens opposition, and saves time.
- **Working group** costs the most time but improves cross-bloc access and trust.
- **Count** is the strongest early swing-vote move and changes no substantive position.
- **Caucus** builds negotiating discipline at the cost of appearing more factional.

No Scene 1 path is a disguised concession. A player should be able to hold their opening objective and still play intelligently.

The next scene, **Orin's Offer**, should be the first place where a genuinely substantive package appears and where the player has to distinguish a policy concession from a procedural or fiscal guarantee.

# Scene 2 — Orin's Offer

## Shared scene setup

Three sittings later, Dessa Orin puts four pages on every delegate's desk.

It is not a Statehood resolution.

It is not merger language.

It does not tell the Convention which map to choose.

That is why everyone reads it.

The proposal says that any resolution preserving Korda as a single political unit should carry a binding transition commitment on infrastructure and public capacity: a published project list, protected funding treatment during the transition, regular progress reporting, and a mechanism requiring the successor government to explain publicly when a listed project slips.

If the Convention instead proposes a territorial split, the same package would require the resolution to say which side inherits each listed obligation and how shared freight and service links continue during transition.

Orin has very carefully not solved the national fiscal equalization dispute. Her paper does not rewrite §12.6, set a national formula, or bind the Legislature beyond whatever authority the eventual constitutional process permits. It does something narrower.

It refuses to let status change become an excuse for the same roads, transfer capacity, and public-service requests to disappear into another queue.

Rell calls it overdue.

Mire calls it incomplete.

Neither calls it irrelevant.

Orin closes her explanation with the line everyone repeats afterward:

"People should know what changes because of the map, and what government still owes them no matter where the line ends up."

### Player prompt

**What do you do with Orin's offer?**

You can make it central to your coalition, narrow it so the Convention does not become a fiscal conference, use it as leverage for something your side wants, or insist that status must be decided before obligations are attached.

This is the first choice where substance and procedure begin to overlap.

# Scene 2 balancing notes

Scene 2 introduces the first substantive package without making the package synonymous with compromise or with a particular status outcome.

- **Champion / Accept** treats infrastructure obligations as valid under any map and builds trust across blocs.
- **Narrow** is a Threll-specific governance move: preserve enforceability while stopping the Convention from becoming a shadow fiscal legislature.
- **Equivalent** is a Kesh-specific bridge into the later autonomy/split question: accepting shared obligations while demanding institutional recognition of corridor distinctness.
- **Leverage / Trade** can be politically effective but damages trust because an obligation framed as universally owed becomes bargaining currency.
- **Status first** is fast and internally coherent for either role, but it spends cross-bloc confidence.

The package does **not** resolve the live national §12.6 equalization dispute. It concerns the Convention's own resolution and transition obligations only.

For ending logic, `infrastructureCommitment` is an internal state variable with values `full`, `narrow`, `conditional`, or `none`. It contributes to a later `broadPackage` determination but does not by itself trigger the Grand Bargain ending. Kesh's `corridorGuarantee` request is likewise an open bargaining position, not an accomplished constitutional term.

The next scene, **The Corridor Line**, is where status itself becomes unavoidable and where a player can choose concession, persuasion, or escalation.
