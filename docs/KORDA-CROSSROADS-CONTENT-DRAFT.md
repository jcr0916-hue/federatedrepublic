# Korda Crossroads — Authored Content Draft

*Status: DRAFT · 260918. Non-canon interactive content. This file is source prose for the future `korda-crossroads.json`; it is not published World canon and does not advance the real Convention.*

*Canon anchors for this draft: the Supreme Court has affirmed the JMC; the stay is dissolved; roughly 87 days remain on the Convention clock; the Convention has voted 25–8 to move its remaining sessions from Verentum into Korda; Dessa Orin, Jalen Mire, and Tomas Rell are established delegates. Exact local venue remains intentionally unnamed.*

---

## Intro screen

### Title
**The Convention**

### Premise
Korda has two certified claims on the same ground and one room in which to reconcile them.

The lake corridor qualified a petition to join Kelvant. The referendum was scheduled. Then a second petition covering all of Korda qualified inside the same constitutional window. The Joint Monitor Council sent both claims into a Territory Convention instead. The corridor challenged that decision. The Supreme Court let it stand.

Now the litigation is over. The Convention has moved from Verentum into Korda. Thirty-three Assembly delegates have about eighty-seven days left to produce one proposed resolution addressing all affected portions together. If they fail, both petitions lapse.

You are one of those delegates.

You do not control the room. You do not control the clock. And even if you win inside the Convention, the voters affected by the resolution still get the final word before the ordinary constitutional process continues.

# Playable Role 1 — Sena Threll

## Picker card

**Sena Threll**  
Interior delegate · Adren region

You spent years routing grain around roads that were always on somebody else's funding list. You came to the Convention believing Korda should remain whole and become a State. The corridor's petition is lawful. You also think it would leave the rest of Korda weaker.

**Starting position:** Whole-Territory Statehood, no special corridor carve-out.

## Full role introduction

The first thing you learn in agricultural logistics is that maps lie.

A road can exist on a map and still be useless six weeks every year. A bridge can be open and still cost a cooperative half a day because the approach cannot take a loaded truck. A federal capacity plan can list your district for four years and still leave you calling three drivers at dawn because somebody has to get grain around the washout.

You did that work before anyone put your name on a ballot.

Dessa Orin did some of it too, later and from the Assembly. She learned how to get the problem into committee records. You learned how to get a shipment to market anyway. You respect her. Some days you also resent how easily a capital can turn years of other people's improvisation into one politician's good sentence.

The lake corridor has a different complaint. Its residents organized a petition to join Kelvant, cleared the threshold, survived the required assessment, and got a referendum date. Then the whole-Territory petition arrived and the Constitution forced both claims into this room. The corridor never got its vote.

You do not think that makes the corridor wrong.

You think it makes the choice dangerous.

If the corridor leaves, inland Korda loses freight, assessed economic activity, and the part of the Territory that received the investment everyone else was told would eventually spread outward. Your district would not have had a vote on the merger. It would have lived with the result.

So you arrive wanting one thing: Korda whole, Korda a State, and no bargain that solves the corridor's grievance by making the interior permanently dependent on it.

The problem is that wanting one thing and having seventeen other delegates want enough of it are not the same skill.

**Starting state — provisional for balancing**
- Interior Support: 58
- Corridor Support: 30
- Swing Votes: 35
- Days Remaining: 87
- Trust: 0

# Playable Role 2 — Davin Kesh

## Picker card

**Davin Kesh**  
Lake-corridor delegate · Varenne Station area

You helped collect signatures for the petition that was supposed to put the corridor's future to a vote. It certified, cleared review, and received a referendum date. Then another petition erased the ballot before anyone could find out whether you would win.

**Starting position:** Preserve corridor distinctness — negotiated merger if possible, entrenched autonomy at minimum.

## Full role introduction

You know exactly how many signatures fit in the metal case under the passenger seat of your old freight van.

You know because for three months you drove that case between depots, market yards, ferry approaches, and warehouse offices whenever the day's hauling left you an hour to spare. You were not the face of the corridor petition. You were one of the people who made sure there was a petition to have a face.

It certified.

The viability assessment cleared it.

The Elections Panel set the vote.

Then, before anyone cast a ballot, the whole of Korda qualified a second petition and the constitutional machinery changed tracks underneath you.

You did not lose a referendum.

That would almost be easier.

You lost the chance to find out.

People inland tell you they were facing a decision that would strip away too much of Korda's freight and tax base without giving them a vote. You understand the argument. You hauled enough inland freight to know those towns are not imaginary lines beyond the corridor. But your voters followed the process available to them. They signed. They waited. They cleared every gate. Now some of the same people who had no vote in the corridor referendum sit in a Convention empowered to decide what replaces it.

The Court says that is constitutional.

Constitutional is not the same thing as settled.

You arrive determined that the corridor will not disappear into a whole-Korda resolution as a footnote. A negotiated split remains your preferred answer. If that cannot command the room, you want structural protections strong enough that the corridor does not spend the next generation being told its distinct interests are merely local complaints.

Thirty-two other delegates have their own version of fairness.

You have eighty-seven days to make enough of them live with yours.

**Starting state — provisional for balancing**
- Interior Support: 42
- Corridor Support: 64
- Swing Votes: 35
- Days Remaining: 87
- Trust: 0

# Scene 1 — First Sitting in Korda

## Shared scene setup

The first sitting after relocation begins without ceremony.

The room is larger than the temporary chamber in Verentum and less polished. The press seats are full anyway. Residents line the back wall until staff stop adding chairs. Outside, people who spent weeks traveling to the capital to watch their own constitutional proceeding are discovering that proximity does not make the arguments simpler.

The clerk reads the Supreme Court disposition into the Convention record, then the relocation vote, then the calendar.

About eighty-seven days remain.

No one has filed a resolution.

That fact hangs in the room longer than the clerk's voice does.

Dessa Orin is three seats to your left. Jalen Mire, from the lake corridor, has a folder open in front of him with the old referendum notices still clipped inside. Tomas Rell has already filled half a page with names and arrows.

The chair asks for motions concerning the Convention's order of business.

This is the first moment since the Court ruling when nobody else has decided what happens next.

### Player prompt

**What do you do first?**

You can force the substantive question into the open, build a cross-bloc working group, spend the opening days counting and persuading quietly, or organize your own side before bargaining begins.

No option requires you to change what you want. The question is how you intend to move the room.

## Fragment set — Sena Threll

### `s1_threll_force`
**Classifier descriptor:** Publicly move to put whole-Territory Statehood on the agenda immediately; force an early debate or test vote without offering corridor concessions.

**Provisional deltas:** interior +10 · corridor -8 · swing +2 · days -4 · trust 0

**Response prose:**

You stand before anyone can build a procedural wall around the morning.

"Put Statehood first."

There is an audible reaction from the corridor seats, but not surprise. Everyone in the room knew where you started.

You keep the motion narrower than some of your own allies wanted. No final vote today. No language pretending the corridor petition never existed. You ask only that the Convention make whole-Territory Statehood the first substantive proposal formally debated.

Rell seconds.

Mire does not.

Orin waits until the chair recognizes her and then asks the question that changes the temperature of the room: whether your motion is intended to establish priority or entitlement.

"Priority," you say.

It costs you nothing with the interior. If anything, delegates who worried the relocation would become weeks of process now have proof that somebody intends to use the clock.

The corridor hears something else: the first local sitting has barely opened and the interior is already trying to decide which claim gets to speak first.

The motion does not settle anything. It does what you wanted.

It makes everyone choose where to sit for the first argument.

---

### `s1_threll_workgroup`
**Classifier descriptor:** Propose a cross-bloc working group or small drafting group before any substantive resolution; seek information and relationships without conceding whole-Territory Statehood.

**Provisional deltas:** interior -2 · corridor +7 · swing +9 · days -7 · trust +1

**Response prose:**

You ask for six chairs.

Not metaphorically. Six actual delegates: two from districts identified with the whole-Territory petition, two from the corridor, two who have managed not to be claimed by either side.

The motion is procedural enough that nobody can call it surrender and political enough that everybody knows what it is for.

"Forty-eight hours," you tell the room. "Not to write a compromise. To write the questions any resolution has to answer."

Mire looks at you for several seconds before nodding.

Rell looks less pleased.

That is useful information.

By the end of the sitting the working group exists, and its mandate is deliberately modest: freight, fiscal transition, representation, public services, and what legal steps follow each possible resolution. No one is authorized to trade away anybody else's position.

Some interior delegates tell you afterward that you have given the corridor a veto before negotiations even begin.

You tell them you have given the corridor a chair.

The difference will matter only if you can keep it that way.

---

### `s1_threll_count`
**Classifier descriptor:** Avoid a public opening move; privately count votes, talk to undecided delegates, and map what might move them while holding the whole-Statehood position.

**Provisional deltas:** interior +3 · corridor +1 · swing +10 · days -5 · trust 0

**Response prose:**

You let three other delegates make speeches.

While they are doing it, you start writing numbers.

Not the meter the press will publish later. The useful numbers: who keeps looking toward Orin before speaking, who applauds Mire's point about the canceled referendum but not his implied conclusion, who talks about Korda as "we" and who talks about their district by name.

Rell catches on first.

"You're counting."

"Obviously."

By lunch you have learned more than the morning debate would have told you.

Several delegates who signed statements sympathetic to whole-Territory Statehood do not want a clean Statehood resolution if the corridor walks out of the process. Two corridor members who defend the old referendum are privately open to remaining in Korda if the settlement changes how infrastructure and local authority are handled. Three members appear to want whichever proposal can survive the eventual referendum.

You have not changed one word of your position.

You have changed your idea of where the votes might come from.

---

### `s1_threll_caucus`
**Classifier descriptor:** Organize the inland/whole-Korda bloc first; consolidate allies, coordinate message, or establish negotiating discipline before engaging the corridor.

**Provisional deltas:** interior +9 · corridor -4 · swing +4 · days -5 · trust 0

**Response prose:**

You do not begin with the corridor.

You begin in a side room with eleven delegates who have spent the last month being described collectively as "the interior" despite representing places that disagree about nearly everything except not wanting to be ignored.

Your pitch is not unity for its own sake.

It is discipline.

No freelancing promises. No delegate tells the press that Statehood is inevitable. No one dismisses the certified corridor petition as a tantrum. And nobody trades away the whole-Territory position before the bloc has decided what it actually needs to protect.

Orin attends and says very little.

Rell approves immediately.

By the time the group breaks, you have something closer to a negotiating bloc than you had that morning.

You also have Mire telling reporters that the first organized act of the relocated Convention was the interior deciding what it would say to the corridor before asking the corridor what it wanted.

Both descriptions are fair.

That is going to be a recurring problem.

## Fragment set — Davin Kesh

### `s1_kesh_force`
**Classifier descriptor:** Publicly demand that the Convention begin from the certified corridor petition or preserve merger as the first substantive option; force early debate without softening the corridor claim.

**Provisional deltas:** interior -8 · corridor +10 · swing +2 · days -4 · trust 0

**Response prose:**

You put the old referendum notice on the desk before you speak.

Not dramatically. Flat. Paper on wood.

"The Convention exists because this was canceled," you say. "So begin there."

Your motion asks the chair to place preservation of the corridor petition's substance first on the agenda: not automatic merger, not a declaration that the Convention is bound by a vote that never happened, but a rule that any resolution must address the certified claim explicitly rather than absorb it into a whole-Korda proposal.

Mire seconds before you finish sitting down.

The interior reaction is immediate.

Rell calls the motion an attempt to give one petition priority over the other after the Court has already said they must be addressed together.

You answer that "together" cannot mean "one of them disappears."

The room divides exactly as everyone expected.

But the question is now unavoidable: whether the corridor petition is merely background to the Convention or one of the things the Convention must preserve in recognizable form.

You have made sure nobody gets to postpone that argument.

---

### `s1_kesh_workgroup`
**Classifier descriptor:** Propose a cross-bloc working group or joint drafting table before pushing merger; seek shared facts and bargaining space while preserving the corridor's position.

**Provisional deltas:** interior +7 · corridor -2 · swing +9 · days -7 · trust +1

**Response prose:**

You propose a table with six seats.

The corridor members nearest you look annoyed before you finish describing it.

That is probably healthy.

Two corridor delegates. Two interior delegates. Two members neither side gets to name as its own. Forty-eight hours to produce a common list of consequences every serious resolution must address: freight, revenue, courts, representation, public services, and what happens to obligations already attached to Korda as a Territory.

"No compromise mandate," you say. "Facts first."

Threll—who has spent the morning looking as if she expected you to open with the old referendum notice held over your head—asks whether merger remains on the table.

"Everything certified remains on the table."

She nods once.

Afterward, one of your corridor colleagues tells you that the interior will use the working group to turn a simple question into twelve complicated ones.

You tell him the question stopped being simple the moment a second petition certified.

He does not like the answer.

Mire does.

For the first time since the Convention began, that distinction feels useful.

---

### `s1_kesh_count`
**Classifier descriptor:** Hold off on a public demand; privately count delegates, identify persuadable interior/swing members, and learn what protections might move them without abandoning corridor distinctness.

**Provisional deltas:** interior +2 · corridor +3 · swing +10 · days -5 · trust 0

**Response prose:**

You do not make a motion.

Several corridor organizers watching from the back of the room will hate that.

You spend the sitting learning who actually needs what.

The loudest whole-Korda speeches are not the problem. Those votes are probably gone unless the room changes dramatically. The useful delegates are the ones who keep attaching conditions to their sentences.

Statehood, **if** the corridor does not hollow out.

Unity, **if** local authority changes.

No merger, **unless** freight access is guaranteed.

No special status, **but** something has to replace the vote that was canceled.

You mark each one.

By the end of the day, you have a list of delegates who may not want the corridor to leave but clearly understand why it tried.

That is not support.

It is better than being treated as a petition that already lost.

---

### `s1_kesh_caucus`
**Classifier descriptor:** Convene the corridor bloc first; coordinate demands, prevent members from bargaining separately, and establish a common floor before negotiating with the rest of Korda.

**Provisional deltas:** interior -4 · corridor +9 · swing +4 · days -5 · trust 0

**Response prose:**

The first real negotiation happens with people who mostly agree with you.

That is intentional.

The corridor delegation meets before the afternoon sitting and discovers quickly that "preserve the corridor claim" means at least four different things.

One delegate wants merger language or nothing.

Another would accept a unified Korda with entrenched local authority.

A third cares less about status than about guaranteeing that the old referendum petition cannot simply vanish from the final record.

Mire listens longer than anyone expects, then says the sentence that becomes the caucus rule:

"We do not have to agree on the destination today. We do have to stop negotiating against ourselves."

You leave with a common floor: no Convention resolution gets corridor support unless it recognizes that the canceled petition represented a lawful, certified claim requiring a concrete answer.

It is not merger.

It is enough to make the interior notice that the corridor has stopped speaking in eight voices.

# Scene 1 balancing notes

These deltas are placeholders, not final numbers. The intended lessons are:

- **Force** strengthens the player's home bloc quickly, sharpens opposition, and saves time.
- **Working group** costs the most time but improves cross-bloc access and trust.
- **Count** is the strongest early swing-vote move and changes no substantive position.
- **Caucus** builds negotiating discipline at the cost of appearing more factional.

No Scene 1 path is a disguised concession. A player should be able to hold their opening objective and still play intelligently.

The next scene, **Orin's Offer**, should be the first place where a genuinely substantive package appears and where the player has to distinguish a policy concession from a procedural or fiscal guarantee.
