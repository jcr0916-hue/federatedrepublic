# Before the Ground Thaws

*By Elias Venn — The Corindal Ledger — proposed Year 14, Month 2*

ASHBRIDGE — In most years the loading yard at the south end of town fills and empties without anyone beyond the valley taking notice. This year the yard is the story, and the calendar is the one deadline nobody can move.

The three mills along the Ash run move most of a year's cut in a short stretch of late winter, when the ground is still firm enough to carry trucks on the back roads and the yard's rail connection can move freight reliably. After the thaw begins, the roads soften, and the road-haulage window closes until the ground firms again in early summer — too late for the spring contracts most of the buyers signed in the autumn. The railway remains usable subject to the carrier's limits on the troubled spur.

This winter, a short section of the branch spur that serves the yard was found to need work: settled ties, a stretch of drainage that fills faster than it drains, and a curve where the ballast has thinned. It is not, the carrier insists, a derailment waiting to happen. It is a question of what the line can carry, and how fast.

Bram Tallow runs one of the three mills that depend on the yard. "I don't have a technical opinion about the track," he said. "I have a stack of contracts and a loader crew that needs to know whether they work in March or July." He has held off committing two crews past the end of the month. "A day I can absorb. A month I cannot. If the yard can't move it, it goes by truck, and the back road won't carry my loads in a thaw."

Sera Hallick, the short-line's freight agent, will not put a date on the repair. "I'll tell a customer what I know, not what they want to hear," she said. The carrier has surveyed the section and priced the work, but the question of who pays has not been settled. The line is privately held; the yard is shared; the three mills split its use by standing agreement rather than by charter. "The track serves three customers," Hallick said. "One wants the work done now and thinks another should carry more of it. That is not an engineering problem."

The county has its own interest, though no one in the yard pretends the spur is the county's to fix. A commissioner, Aldis Reeve, has asked the mills and the carrier to consider what heavy loads will do to the unimproved road that parallels the track once the thaw starts. "We can't make the road carry more than it can," Reeve said. "We can tell them what it will look like afterward, and let them argue about who pays for that instead." He stopped short of announcing any county action.

In the yard, the practical arithmetic is simpler than the legal one. A loader, asked what a lost week would mean, glanced at the empty flatcars lined against the fence. "It means we stack it and wait," he said. "Timber doesn't spoil. The people who buy it don't wait."

The thaw will arrive on its own schedule. The work on the spur is not yet scheduled, the split of its cost is not yet agreed, and the contracts already signed are not going to renegotiate themselves. For now the town is doing what it always does in a season like this: watching the weather, counting loads, and arguing in low voices about who owes what before the ground gives way.
