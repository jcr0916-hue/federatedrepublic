The Unremovable

Year 5. Davan Iskal's residency certification is revoked for documented fraud, and a final removal order issues. His state of origin has ceased to function; no third country will accept him; the one government that offers to take him is the reason he cannot be sent. He is also the subject of a security assessment. §6.3.a was written for exactly this man — the case with no good outcome, where the constitution's floor is actually tested.

§6.3.a Unexecuted Removal Orders §1.21 Non-Refoulement §6.2 Enforcement, Removal, and Appeals §1.11 Habeas Corpus §6.3 Asylum and Protection

Every immigration system produces a small number of cases it cannot finish. A person is ordered removed — lawfully, after process, for cause — and then the order meets the world: the destination state will not issue travel documents, or has ceased to exist as a functioning authority, or would persecute the person on arrival, which §1.21 prohibits absolutely and non-derogably. Systems without a rule for this remainder drift into one of two answers. They detain indefinitely, warehousing people whose only remaining offense is being unreturnable. Or they deport anyway and stop asking what happens on the far end. §6.3.a exists because the Republic's founders had seen both answers and prohibited both.

Davan Iskal entered the Republic in Year 2 through the two-stage system: sponsored by Evantine, federally certified, credentialed. In Year 4 a routine certification audit found the fraud — his identity documents were authentic, but the employment and residency history supporting his certification category had been fabricated by the brokerage that arranged his application, with his participation. Revocation proceedings ran their full course: statutory grounds, judicial challenge, appeal, with counsel appointed at public expense under §6.2. In Year 5, Month 1, the removal order became final. That was the last routine event in the case.

The Order Meets the World

Iskal's state of origin had collapsed into contested administration two years earlier; no authority there could issue the acceptance a removal requires. Two third countries with historical ties declined. The one government that formally offered to receive him controlled the region his family had fled — and the statutory independent asylum adjudicative body, on his §1.21 claim, found a genuine risk of persecution on arrival. The protection determination barred that route absolutely and conferred federal legal residency under §6.3 without State sponsorship or federal certification. §1.21 admits no exception and §1.19.a lists it among the six non-derogable rights: not for fraud, not for security, not for the administrative convenience of finishing a file.

What sharpened the case was the second file. The federal certification review had flagged, and an intelligence assessment documented, Iskal's pre-entry association with an armed network in the collapsed state — the same association, as it happened, that made his return there lethal. The Republic thus held a man it had lawfully ordered removed, could not remove, and assessed as a security concern. In most constitutional orders, that combination resolves quietly into a detention cell with no calendar. §6.3.a is written against the quiet resolution: a person whose removal cannot be executed remains at liberty.

National Record System — Federal District Court, Evantine — Year 5, Month 4
ORDER — Habeas Petition, In re Iskal
Conducted under §1.11 and §6.3.a — Unexecuted Removal Orders

The court finds: (1) the removal order is final and cannot presently be executed; execution to the offering state would violate §1.21; (2) the government identifies no pending criminal charge and no other lawful basis for continued detention; (3) §6.3.a provides that a person whose removal cannot be executed remains at liberty, subject to all applicable laws of the jurisdiction in which the person resides.

The petition is granted. The petitioner shall be released and shall comply with the registration and reporting requirements the Legislature has established. The security assessment may inform lawful investigation; it is not a basis for detention under this order.

— Federal District Court

The Floor

The government argued that the security file justified holding Iskal until some country accepted him. The court asked what authority permitted that. §6.3.a answers in its first sentence: a person whose removal cannot be executed remains at liberty. It grants no power to detain for the failure of a removal, and the security assessment did not change the answer. An assessment is a reason to investigate, and Iskal remained subject to every law that applies to anyone else in Evantine, including the criminal law. It is not a reason to hold a man whose only remaining offense is being unreturnable.

Year 5, Month 4 — Release
The security file is real, and it does not change the rule.

Iskal was released under the registration and reporting requirements the Legislature had set by statute. The requirements were reasonable: periodic check-ins at a designated office and notice of any change of residence. The government's concern did not disappear. It moved into the channels the Constitution leaves open: lawful investigation, and prosecution if evidence of a crime ever appeared. None did.

The government did not appeal. The petition asked a question with one answer under the text, and the government had no authority to cite on the other side.

The Status

Iskal’s protection determination had conferred federal legal residency under §6.3, independently of the fraudulent dual-gate certification that had been revoked. After release he was free to work and to live in Evantine under Article I protections. Neither fraud nor a security assessment permitted refoulement, and neither permitted detention without a lawful basis.

What This Scenario Tests

§6.3.a is the constitution operating at its point of maximum temptation. Iskal is the least sympathetic figure the immigration system produces — a proven fraud with a security file — and the provision’s protection does not depend on sympathy. A person whose removal cannot be executed remains at liberty; the sentence contains no exception for hard cases because it was written for the hard cases. A floor that holds only for the deserving is not a floor.

The provision’s architecture is simple. It does not build a detention system with a calendar. It removes the power to detain for a failed removal and leaves the government with what it has against anyone else: investigation, prosecution on evidence, and reasonable registration and reporting. Nothing about Iskal’s release required official mercy or political risk.

And the pairing with §1.21 closes the system honestly. Non-refoulement without §6.3.a creates the warehouse; §6.3.a without non-refoulement invites the quiet flight to the persecuting state. Together they force the Republic to hold its hardest case inside the law, because the alternatives are the two things the constitution decided the Republic would not be.
