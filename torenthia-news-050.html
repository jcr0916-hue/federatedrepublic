The Difference Between Wrong and Not Enough

Three outlets covered Wednesday's Executive Monitor certification this week. All three led with the same sentence: no failure was found. None of them stayed long enough to explain why that sentence and "everything is fine" are not the same claim. That's the actual lesson, so let's do it properly.

Rosalind Kade Show Your Work · Selvane Year 13, Month 9

I've spent three years teaching a unit on constitutional design to students who mostly want to know when the bell rings. Every year, one lesson lands harder than the rest, and it's not one of the exciting ones — not the emergency powers, not the removal procedures. It's the boring one: the difference between a rule being broken and a rule not being enough. This week gave me an example better than anything in my usual slides, so I'm starting this blog with it instead of waiting for the syllabus to catch up.

Here is what happened, stripped of everything except the mechanics.

What the Formula Actually Promises

Under §12.8, the Legislature has to maintain a mechanism ensuring every State and Territory has enough money to meet its basic obligations. Once a year, the Executive Monitor checks whether that mechanism is working the way the law says it should work, and publishes the answer. If the Monitor finds it's broken — genuinely malfunctioning, not doing what its own rules say — the Legislature has 90 days to respond. That's the whole mechanical promise. Notice what it does not promise: it does not promise the outcomes will be good. It promises the process will run as designed.

This week's certification found the process running exactly as designed. No malfunction. Fenn was right to say so, and nobody is disputing it.

What the Numbers Say Anyway

Here's the part that would normally stop there, except this time it didn't. The Executive Monitor's office was asked — specifically, by the joint committee, for the first time on record — to also publish how long a capacity request actually takes to clear once it's filed. Not whether it clears. How long.

The answer: for Territories, the wait has roughly doubled in four years. For already-disadvantaged States, it's barely moved. The formula itself can't see this, because it only ever records one bit of information — funded, or not funded — and a request that took six years shows up identically to one that took six months.

So: the mechanism is not broken. And the mechanism is also, on this evidence, clearly not doing enough. Both of those sentences are true at the same time, and the constitution was built to let them both be true without contradiction.

Why the Document Is Built This Way on Purpose

I think the instinct most people have here is that this looks like a loophole — like the formula is dodging accountability by hiding behind its own narrow definition of success. I'd push back on that, gently, the way I'd push back on a student who thinks a poorly-designed test is the same thing as a cheating student.

A mechanism that automatically triggers a mandatory legislative response every time the underlying outcomes look bad isn't actually more accountable — it's a mechanism that hands enormous, undefined power to whoever gets to decide what "looks bad" means. §12.8 doesn't do that. It ties the automatic, forcing consequence to something narrow and provable — did the mechanism do what it said it would do — and leaves everything else, the genuinely hard argument about whether the formula itself is good enough, to the Legislature, in public, with the data now sitting in front of everyone rather than buried inside a report nobody outside the committee would ever have read.

That's not the system failing to notice a problem. That's the system being honest about which kind of problem it caught automatically, and which kind it's handing back to elected people to argue about where voters can watch them do it.

The Actual Homework

The joint committee now has real numbers instead of two people's instincts. What they do with those numbers — whether they write a new formula, whether Senator Fenn's states agree to one, whether it goes nowhere for another year — is not written down anywhere in §12.8, because that decision was never supposed to be automatic. It was always supposed to be theirs, made badly or well, in front of everyone.

Show your work. That's the whole blog, really. I'll be back when something else is worth walking through properly.
