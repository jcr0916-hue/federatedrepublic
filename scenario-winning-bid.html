The Winning Bid

Canonical history: Caldenmere’s premium shoreline bid loses to Aldenmere’s qualified, lower-cost Verentum proposal. Two court challenges test compliance and finality after bidding closes.

§18.2 Seat of Government§18.1 The Use Doctrine

Choosing the federal seat gave the States an opportunity to host the Republic’s institutions. Harren considered bidding, but its voters were not interested. Caldenmere wanted the honor and put forward a shoreline location near Calden, pairing its maritime and financial setting with a site intended to give the national government a distinguished home.

The setting carried a premium. Shoreline land was expensive, and the additional land and development costs made Caldenmere’s proposal substantially dearer than Aldenmere’s offer at Verentum. Both bids met the minimum requirements published in advance. The selection authority largely distinguished them on cost within the lawful statutory process. Aldenmere won with a materially cheaper qualified proposal.

First challenge — published requirements
A qualified bid can lose on cost.

Caldenmere challenged the selection before the Supreme Court. Federal §18.2 gave a rejected bidder a challenge on the ground that the selected bid had not satisfied the published requirements. It did not promise the losing State a judicial reranking of every desirable feature. Caldenmere’s case therefore had to establish a defect in Verentum’s compliance; its preference for the shoreline proposal could not supply one.

The Court found that Verentum met every published minimum requirement and upheld the process. Cost could lawfully distinguish among qualified bids. The decision did not declare Caldenmere’s site unsuitable: it left the valid, cheaper selection in place. Caldenmere lost its first challenge.

Second challenge — a closed competition
Paying the premium could not reopen bidding.

Caldenmere then offered to pay the full additional land and development premium itself, so the federal government would no longer bear the extra shoreline cost. The State returned to court seeking to reopen the matter on those revised terms.

By then, the bidding period had closed and the award had been made. The Court held that Caldenmere could not resubmit a materially revised offer after closure and force reconsideration of the completed competition. This was finality under the statutory selection process, rather than a separate deadline written into §18.2. Otherwise a losing bidder could revise its price after seeing the winner’s terms. The second challenge failed, and Aldenmere’s selection stood.

The outcome settled the federal host without combining the two capitals. Terenne, on the coast, remained Aldenmere’s State capital; Verentum became the federal seat within Aldenmere. Under §§18.1–18.2, the Republic received Operational Use Rights over the designated federal footprint and exclusive control of federal functions there. It acquired no territorial sovereignty; Aldenmere retained full sovereignty outside the footprint, and residents within it retained their Article I rights.

The same history reads differently from each State. For Aldenmere, it validates a qualified, affordable offer and the stability of the resulting award. For Caldenmere, it records the ambition behind the shoreline bid and the limits of a late attempt to cure its cost disadvantage.
