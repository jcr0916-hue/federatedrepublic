The Three Hours

Year 6. The Legat Consul dies during an NRS outage. The succession happens immediately. The public record catches up three hours later.

§2.9 Legat Consul Succession§7.15 Authority by Constitutional Operation§10.1 National Record System

At 2:13 AM, the Legat Consul died unexpectedly during emergency surgery. At the same moment, a regional network failure had taken the NRS public interface and publication channel offline. The outage began eleven minutes earlier and would last until 5:47 AM.

The Senate Speaker was awake within minutes. At 2:31 AM, military command requested authorization to reposition two surveillance aircraft after a foreign vessel crossed a treaty monitoring line. Staff counsel raised the obvious question: could the Senate Speaker act before the succession appeared on the NRS?

The Office Changed at 2:13 AM

§2.9 + §7.15 — The trigger creates the authority
The Constitution does not leave an office vacant while the database catches up.

Full succession activated at the Legat Consul's death. The Senate Speaker held the office from that moment. No oath, acceptance, appointment instrument, announcement, or publication was required to make the transfer effective.

§7.15 makes the general rule explicit: when authority is conferred, transferred, or assumed by constitutional operation, it vests when the constitutional condition occurs unless the Constitution expressly makes publication or another implementing act a condition of effect.

The 2:31 AM Decision

The new Legat Consul approved the aircraft repositioning at 2:34 AM. The order identified the succession trigger, the time of death, and the NRS outage. Military command executed it.

A later lawsuit argued that the order was void because the NRS did not yet show the Senate Speaker as Legat Consul. The argument reversed the constitutional relationship between authority and recordkeeping.

§10.1 — Authoritative record, not constitutive act
The NRS proves and preserves the change. It ordinarily does not create it.

Once service returned, the succession record was entered at 5:52 AM and identified 2:13 AM as the constitutional effective time. The 2:34 AM order was then published in the ordinary sequence. The record did not backdate authority; it recorded authority that had already vested.

The result would be different only where the Constitution expressly makes publication a condition of effect. In those provisions, publication is part of the trigger. Here, death itself was the trigger.

Why the Distinction Matters

If the NRS created authority, a technical outage could create a temporary constitutional vacuum precisely when continuity matters most. If officials could ignore the NRS entirely, public verification would become discretionary. The Constitution rejects both extremes.

What This Scenario Tests

§7.15 separates constitutional authority from administrative recording. The triggering event determines when authority changes. Article X makes the NRS the authoritative public record of that change and requires the record to be made as soon as possible.

The three-hour gap therefore produces no three-hour vacancy. It produces a three-hour delay in public recording. The successor may lawfully act during that interval, and the NRS must later show the actual constitutional moment of succession.
